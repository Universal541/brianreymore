*An agricultural love story, an indictment of manufactured ignorance, and the peculiar human addiction to bullshit.*

## Somewhere Beyond the Madness

There will come a time when our descendants look back upon a particularly embarrassing period of human history and struggle to understand how an ostensibly intelligent species became so thoroughly frightened of a flowering plant that it established entire bureaucracies devoted to persecuting it. They will examine government records, newspaper archives, congressional testimony, agricultural publications, medical literature, and the astonishing collection of cinematic nonsense we once mistook for public education. Somewhere in the process, one of them will inevitably look up from the historical record and ask what the fuck was wrong with us.

It will be a fair question.

For nearly a century, governments have criminalized, marginalized, misrepresented, restricted, confiscated, and regulated cannabis with a degree of institutional enthusiasm that would have been considerably more useful had it been directed toward understanding the plant. We have imprisoned people for growing it, possessing it, transporting it, distributing it, and occasionally for being unfortunate enough to occupy the same automobile as somebody who had it. We have interrupted agricultural industries, obstructed medical research, divided families, destroyed businesses, and saddled generations of citizens with criminal records for participating in a relationship humanity has maintained with this plant for thousands of years.

And before anybody starts congratulating us on having emerged from that particular dark age, we should probably examine the machinery presently operating in its place.

In much of America, the old prohibitionist apparatus has been supplemented by a spectacularly convoluted regulatory apparatus. Cannabis may be permissible under the laws of one jurisdiction and prohibited under those of another. Federal law remains at odds with portions of state law. Cultivators and manufacturers face licensing requirements, testing mandates, security requirements, surveillance systems, inventory tracking, transportation restrictions, banking complications, and a collection of administrative obligations capable of making an otherwise competent agricultural entrepreneur reconsider the relative simplicity of raising goats.

Even industrial hemp, a commodity whose cultivation should be as natural a subject for agricultural education as flax, corn, or sunflowers, remains entangled in this nonsense.

We haven't liberated the plant. We've negotiated its terms of parole.

I have personally used cannabis for more than forty years without experiencing what I would identify as a cannabis-related health setback or an addiction problem. I am hardly alone. My experience is not a comprehensive clinical study, nor have I ever pretended it was, but it is part of an enormous body of lived human experience that has been routinely marginalized whenever it inconvenienced the prevailing political narrative.

The consumption of cannabis does not constitute a disease, a moral deficiency, or evidence of addiction. Some people develop problematic relationships with cannabis, just as people develop destructive relationships with alcohol, gambling, food, sex, money, power, and a remarkable assortment of otherwise ordinary aspects of human existence. Understanding those relationships requires understanding human beings. Criminalizing the objects around which those relationships develop is a spectacularly inefficient substitute for doing so.

The future I hope to see is one in which humanity has finally overcome its two most persistent cannabis-related addictions: the genuinely problematic consumption experienced by some individuals, and the much more widespread institutional addiction to bullshit.

Judging by the historical record, the second may prove considerably more difficult to treat.

## The Plant Was Here First

Our relationship with cannabis is older than recorded civilization. Genetic research published in *Science Advances* in 2021 places the domestication of Cannabis sativa in early Neolithic East Asia, with genomic estimates suggesting a relationship extending back approximately twelve thousand years. Archaeological evidence, historical records, and the extraordinary geographic distribution of cultivated cannabis support a history of human interaction stretching across millennia.

What makes this relationship particularly interesting is that we haven't merely been growing cannabis for a very long time. We have been influencing its evolution, while cannabis has influenced the development of human societies.

Through cultivation, selection, transportation, and breeding, humans encouraged the development of plants exhibiting characteristics useful to particular communities. Some plants produced desirable fibers; others produced desirable seeds, oils, flowers, or resins. Geography, climate, agricultural practices, cultural preferences, and human ingenuity gradually shaped the diversity of cannabis we recognize today. The plant, in return, supplied materials, nutrition, medicines, intoxicants, and cultural experiences that became embedded in the development of human civilization.

This is a relationship of reciprocal influence. The genetic evolution of a domesticated plant and the cultural, technological, and economic evolution of the species cultivating it are profoundly intertwined. Whether particular human populations experienced identifiable genetic adaptations specifically attributable to cannabis is a separate and considerably less established question, but the larger evolutionary relationship is plainly worthy of investigation.

Entire empires have risen and collapsed during our association with this plant. Religions have emerged, languages have developed and disappeared, continents have been explored, and generations of human beings have transferred agricultural knowledge from their parents to their children. Throughout that history, somebody somewhere was growing cannabis because somebody somewhere had discovered another useful thing to do with it.

Hemp fibers became rope, clothing, sails, fishing nets, paper, and working materials. Its seeds became food. Its oils became household and industrial resources. Its flowers and resins became part of medicinal preparations, spiritual practices, ceremonial traditions, recreational gatherings, and the infinitely diverse assortment of things human beings do to explore consciousness.

In 2019, researchers examining the Jirzankal cemetery in the eastern Pamirs published chemical evidence of cannabis having been burned in wooden braziers during mortuary ceremonies approximately 2,500 years ago. The material showed evidence consistent with cannabis possessing relatively elevated levels of psychoactive compounds. We cannot reconstruct precisely what those experiences meant to the people participating in them, but we can establish that cannabis occupied a place within their ceremonial lives long before anybody had invented a narcotics bureau.

Apparently, our ancestors were capable of developing spiritually and culturally meaningful relationships with cannabis without first consulting a parliamentary subcommittee.

Cannabis became part of humanity's folklore because it became part of humanity's life. Its cultural significance accumulated through generations of ordinary human encounters, and much of that significance survives in places that formal histories have historically been rather poor at finding: kitchens, workshops, farms, fishing communities, traditional healing practices, family stories, and the knowledge carried by people whose names never appeared in books.

It is here that I would like you to meet Minerva.

## Minerva Mayhem McCoy's Medicine Cabinet

Somewhere deep in the Appalachian Mountains, beyond the last reasonably dependable stretch of road, lives a woman named Minerva Mayhem McCoy.

Minerva is fictional, although the people she represents are very much real.

Her family has occupied those mountains for more generations than anybody presently living can reliably account for. The house was constructed largely from materials harvested within walking distance. Its furniture was built by people who understood wood well enough to recognize its character before the first cut was made. Water comes from a spring, heat from a fire, food from a garden and the surrounding forest. Her kitchen smells faintly of woodsmoke, beeswax, dried herbs, rendered animal fat, and earth, the latter owing in no small part to the fact that the floor is made of the stuff.

A proper fucking kitchen, if you ask me.

There are jars on shelves, bundles of plants hanging from the rafters, wooden spoons polished by generations of hands, and an assortment of containers whose contents Minerva knows perfectly well without requiring the assistance of labels. She possesses an understanding of the seasons, the soil, the local plants, and the animals inhabiting the surrounding mountains that would take most of us several lifetimes of weekend workshops to begin acquiring.

People know where to find her. When somebody has an aching joint, a persistent skin irritation, trouble sleeping, or an affliction for which the local remedies have proven inadequate, they occasionally make their way up the mountain. Minerva welcomes them into her home, settles them near the fire, and listens. While she considers their predicament and prepares whatever she believes might help, she may entertain them with an unsolicited story about some distant relative's adventures during a war, a winter that nearly killed everybody, or an unfortunate incident involving a mule, a church deacon, and a barrel of fermented something-or-other.

By the time her visitor leaves, they have received something considerably more substantial than the contents of a jar.

Minerva belongs to a tradition of human knowledge that deserves far more reverence than modern civilization generally affords it. Anyone familiar with the *Foxfire* series, which grew from the efforts of Appalachian high school students beginning in the 1960s to document the knowledge of their elders, will recognize something of the world she inhabits. Those interviews preserved extraordinary accounts of cabin construction, farming, woodworking, hunting, food preservation, herbal remedies, crafts, oral histories, and the resourcefulness of people who had learned to live with the land rather than merely purchase the products of its exploitation.

These people knew a thing or two about a thing or three.

Their knowledge was neither primitive nor simple. Much of it was extraordinarily sophisticated, acquired through prolonged observation, experimentation, necessity, and the accumulated experience of generations. A person who can construct a weather-resistant home from standing timber, identify useful medicinal plants, preserve enough food to survive a winter, make furniture that will outlive their grandchildren, and sustain a family without electricity or municipal services possesses a formidable education, regardless of whether an accredited institution has printed their name on a piece of paper.

People like Minerva also occupied a recognizable place within their communities as trusted healers, custodians of traditional knowledge, and repositories of practical wisdom. The particulars differed enormously across cultures, and the healing traditions of Indigenous peoples should be understood on their own terms, but the larger human institution of the community healer is nearly universal.

Minerva is one of its descendants.

Some of the remedies she prepares work remarkably well. Some may derive their benefits from chemical interactions that modern medicine has yet to fully understand. Others may involve psychological, environmental, physiological, or interpersonal mechanisms operating together. The warmth of her home, the attention she provides, the confidence her visitors have in her, and the relationship between their expectations and their bodies may all contribute to what happens.

I am interested in those questions. I am considerably less interested in the intellectual fashion of dismissing the experience altogether because nobody has yet assembled a sufficiently expensive laboratory around it.

The absence of a published study is a description of the state of published research. It is not a comprehensive description of reality.

Cannabis occupied an important place in the inherited medicine cabinets of numerous cultures, and the experiences associated with those preparations deserve investigation on their merits. Folk knowledge is not rendered useless by its inability to explain itself in the terminology of molecular biology. Often, it is precisely that inability that should make molecular biologists curious.

Minerva may not know what an endocannabinoid receptor is. She may not know the molecular structure of cannabidiol. She may be entirely unfamiliar with the terminology of pharmacokinetics, receptor agonism, and inflammatory signaling pathways.

Nevertheless, when people return to her kitchen because something she gave them helped, they are contributing to an archive of human observation whose value should not be determined exclusively by its compatibility with contemporary institutional procedures.

Minerva's medicine cabinet deserves a place in the history of medicine.

Her knowledge deserves a place in its future.

## Before the Pharmacist Became the Gatekeeper

The medicinal relationship between humanity and cannabis extends far beyond the fictional Appalachian kitchen.

In nineteenth-century India, Irish physician William Brooke O'Shaughnessy investigated existing preparations of Indian hemp and documented observations concerning their application to several medical conditions, including painful spasms and convulsive disorders. His 1843 publication, *On the Preparations of the Indian Hemp, or Gunjah*, is a particularly interesting historical artifact because it records a physician investigating knowledge that already existed within the culture in which he was working.

He did not invent cannabis medicine. He encountered it, examined it, experimented with it, and introduced portions of that knowledge to Western medical audiences.

Cannabis preparations subsequently occupied a recognized place within American and European medicine. Cannabis entered the United States Pharmacopoeia in 1850 and remained there until 1942. Pharmaceutical manufacturers produced cannabis extracts and tinctures, physicians prescribed them, and pharmacists dispensed them. Their customers were people suffering from the ordinary collection of ailments that have afflicted human beings throughout history.

The broader medicine cabinet of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries would horrify a modern prohibitionist. Alongside cannabis preparations were medicines containing opium, morphine, cocaine, and eventually heroin. Bayer began marketing heroin in 1898. Cocaine became an important local anesthetic and also appeared in commercial medicinal preparations. Opium-based products occupied an established, if increasingly controversial, place in household medicine.

These substances had useful pharmacological properties and, particularly in the cases of cocaine and opioids, very substantial potential for harm and dependence. Their historical medical legitimacy did not disappear merely because their risks became better understood. Cocaine still has limited medical applications as a local anesthetic, and diamorphine, the pharmaceutical name for heroin, remains an accepted medical treatment in some countries.

The interesting historical question is how societies came to distinguish between the respectable patient consuming a preparation supplied by a physician and the supposedly disreputable individual consuming a pharmacologically related substance outside an approved institutional arrangement.

Often, the chemistry was considerably less important to that distinction than the social standing of the person consuming it.

America's Harrison Narcotics Tax Act of 1914 established federal controls over opium and coca products well before the Marihuana Tax Act of 1937. Earlier public campaigns had already associated particular substances with particular racial and immigrant populations. Cannabis prohibition drew upon those techniques, expanded their reach, and helped cultivate a public appetite for increasingly theatrical declarations of chemical evil.

That appetite would eventually feed a much broader war on drugs.

The morality train did not originate at the cannabis station, but it picked up a considerable number of passengers there.

## The Curious Case of the Newly Discovered Human

In 1964, researchers Raphael Mechoulam and Yechiel Gaoni elucidated the structure of delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol, commonly known as THC. Subsequent research identified cannabinoid receptors, followed in 1992 by the discovery of anandamide and in 1995 by the identification of another important endogenous cannabinoid, 2-arachidonoylglycerol, more mercifully known as 2-AG.

The significance of these discoveries extended far beyond cannabis. Scientists were uncovering components of an extraordinarily complex physiological signaling system already operating within the human body.

The endocannabinoid system participates in numerous biological processes, including aspects of pain perception, immune function, appetite, memory, stress responses, metabolism, and cellular signaling. Its discovery opened avenues of investigation into human physiology that had comparatively little to do with getting stoned and an enormous amount to do with understanding how living organisms maintain and regulate themselves.

After decades of telling human beings that cannabis represented an unnatural corruption of the body and mind, scientists discovered that the human body had been conducting its own cannabinoid-related business without obtaining congressional approval.

Imagine the indignity.

An entire biological signaling system had apparently escaped the attention of the Federal Bureau of Narcotics. Nobody had applied for a license. No government inspector had verified the purity of its products. The individual human body had been manufacturing endogenous cannabinoids on its own premises, entirely without the security fencing, surveillance equipment, and inventory tracking requirements that we would eventually impose upon farmers growing the plant that helped us discover it.

The audacity of the fucking pancreas, among other organs.

The existence of the endocannabinoid system does not automatically establish that consuming plant-derived cannabinoids will improve every physiological process in which that system participates. What it does establish is that humanity possesses an immensely interesting biological system whose investigation was advanced substantially through our exploration of cannabis.

It also raises a rather uncomfortable historical question: how much sooner might we have begun understanding these processes had we spent the preceding decades encouraging investigation rather than prosecuting curiosity?

We cannot measure the full extent of knowledge we failed to acquire. The discoveries prevented by an absence of investigation do not appear in the scientific literature. There are no patent records for inventions nobody was permitted to pursue, no clinical results for experiments nobody was allowed to conduct, and no biographies of the scientists who might have made discoveries had their professional paths not been redirected by political interference.

The prohibitionist institutions that interrupted this work have never been required to account adequately for those losses.

Perhaps they should be.

## The Man Who Renamed a Plant

By the early twentieth century, Americans knew hemp. Farmers knew hemp. Physicians knew cannabis. Pharmacists knew cannabis preparations. Manufacturers knew fiber. None of these things arrived in America as frightening new discoveries in the 1930s.

But *marihuana* arrived wearing an entirely different costume.

Harry Jacob Anslinger, a veteran of the machinery of alcohol prohibition, became the first commissioner of the Federal Bureau of Narcotics in 1930. He was also married to Martha Denniston, whose family connections extended into the powerful Mellon banking dynasty. Andrew Mellon, then Secretary of the Treasury, was instrumental in establishing the new federal narcotics administration under his department and in Anslinger's appointment to lead it.

The institutional proximity alone deserves considerably more scrutiny than it typically receives in introductory accounts of cannabis prohibition.

Mellon was among the most influential financiers and industrial figures in American history. William Randolph Hearst controlled an enormous newspaper empire capable of shaping public perception on a national scale. Lammot du Pont and the DuPont organization occupied increasingly influential positions within the chemical and synthetic-materials industries. Petroleum, chemical manufacturing, timber, paper production, banking, and industrial materials were transforming the American economy during precisely the period in which cannabis was being transformed from a familiar agricultural and medicinal resource into an object of national suspicion.

Jack Herer, in *The Emperor Wears No Clothes*, devoted considerable attention to the convergence of these interests. His work helped introduce generations of readers to a largely forgotten industrial history and raised questions concerning the potential commercial motivations underlying hemp's marginalization.

Those questions have often been met with demands for a definitive documentary record establishing every private conversation, financial relationship, and commercial intention among the people involved.

I would be delighted to examine that record.

I would also like to examine the records that no longer exist, the conversations that were never committed to paper, the financial arrangements that remain inadequately investigated, and the competing economic opportunities that were eliminated before they could develop sufficient political influence to defend themselves.

History is an extraordinarily interesting thing when we stop pretending that the surviving paperwork constitutes the entirety of the experience.

What can we establish? We can establish that the federal narcotics bureaucracy acquired greater authority through cannabis prohibition. We can establish that influential newspaper publishers disseminated sensational stories concerning cannabis and its supposed consequences. We can establish that industrial hemp possessed recognized commercial applications at a time when competing materials industries were expanding. We can establish that the medical profession's principal representative at the congressional hearings opposed the proposed legislation. We can establish that generations of farmers, patients, manufacturers, and researchers subsequently encountered profound obstacles to continuing their work.

We can also establish that the institutions benefiting from the disappearance of potential competition were considerably better positioned to influence government policy than the people whose opportunities were disappearing.

There is something worth investigating here beyond the personal intentions of Harry Anslinger.

Even if we were to discover that nobody ever convened a meeting specifically dedicated to destroying the hemp industry, the concentration of political authority, commercial influence, media ownership, and financial power within this relatively small constellation of American institutions would remain worthy of examination.

Who benefited? Federal narcotics administrators acquired jurisdiction and institutional longevity. Politicians acquired an inexpensive demonstration of moral leadership. Newspaper publishers acquired sensational material. Established industries continued their expansion into markets in which agricultural materials might otherwise have become more formidable competitors.

Who paid? Farmers, patients, researchers, consumers, manufacturers, marginalized communities, and generations of people who would never even learn what had been taken away from them.

The surviving record answers a considerable portion of those questions. Where it doesn't, I would prefer that we continue asking them rather than treating the absence of a conveniently signed confession as an instruction to stop investigating.

We are examining powerful human institutions, after all, not trying to determine whether the duck in the pond has submitted sufficient documentation establishing its duckness.

## Congress Meets a Plant

During the congressional hearings concerning the Marihuana Tax Act of 1937, Dr. William C. Woodward, legislative counsel for the American Medical Association, appeared before the House Committee on Ways and Means and raised objections to the proposed legislation.

One of those objections concerned the very terminology under which the bill was being presented. The medical profession knew the substance as cannabis. Others knew it as Indian hemp. Agricultural and commercial interests understood its various products according to their familiar uses. Woodward observed that the unfamiliar terminology had contributed to hempseed dealers failing to recognize the implications of the legislation for their own businesses until remarkably late in the process.

Think about the implications of that for a moment.

The public had been exposed to an increasingly sensational narrative about a foreign-sounding substance called marihuana. A substantial portion of that public possessed little reason to associate the frightening substance in the newspapers with the familiar medicines, fibers, seed products, and agricultural practices they already understood.

The resulting confusion extended into Congress itself.

When the bill came before the House, Representative Bertrand Snell asked Sam Rayburn to explain what it concerned. Rayburn's response included the following extraordinary admission:

> I believe it is a narcotic of some kind.

There it is, preserved for posterity.

A man helping move legislation through the United States House of Representatives, legislation whose consequences would extend across generations of American agricultural, medical, industrial, and criminal justice history, believed that the substance under consideration was a narcotic of some kind.

Of some kind.

Rayburn could apparently locate sufficient conviction to support the legislation without possessing enough familiarity with its subject to explain precisely what it was.

The situation becomes even more astonishing when, during the subsequent proceedings, a member of the Ways and Means Committee represented the American Medical Association as supporting the legislation. Woodward's actual testimony demonstrates otherwise.

The congressional record does not reveal the private understanding of every legislator voting on the bill, but it does reveal something sufficiently disturbing: the people responsible for explaining the legislation demonstrated confusion about its subject and misrepresented the position of an important organization whose representative had appeared specifically to oppose it.

So much for informed consent.

Civilization had spent thousands of years accumulating experience with cannabis. Harry Anslinger and the sensationalist press spent a comparatively brief period teaching Americans to fear it. Congress, apparently unable to distinguish the botanical subject of its own legislation from the monster described in the newspapers, devoted mere minutes of floor debate to deciding what future generations would be permitted to do with that inheritance.

Thousands of years of human knowledge were subordinated to the convictions of men who hadn't bothered to establish what the fuck they were talking about.

And we continued calling the resulting legislation public policy.

## Prohibition by Paperwork

The Marihuana Tax Act did not simply declare cannabis illegal. Its mechanism was more administratively sophisticated and considerably more instructive.

It imposed registration requirements, occupational taxes, transfer taxes, and associated documentation obligations upon people engaging in activities involving cannabis. Certain portions of the plant, including mature stalks, fibers derived from those stalks, seed oil, and sterilized seed, were exempted from its definition of marihuana. The cultivation and transfer of the plant itself, however, were brought within a restrictive federal framework.

Tax stamps existed and were occasionally issued. The Smithsonian Institution preserves examples, including one issued to a physician during the early 1960s. Their rarity, together with the underlying registration and tax requirements, contributed to the legislation functioning as a de facto prohibition.

This is important because governments do not always need to prohibit an activity explicitly in order to effectively eliminate it. They can make participation administratively burdensome, financially prohibitive, legally hazardous, and sufficiently uncertain that most reasonable people abandon the endeavor.

The distinction between a prohibition and a regulatory obstacle course becomes somewhat academic when neither allows you to reach the other end.

In 1969, the United States Supreme Court addressed one particularly grotesque feature of this arrangement in *Leary v. United States*. The Court ruled that compliance with the Act's transfer-tax provisions could expose an individual to self-incrimination, and that invoking the Fifth Amendment provided a defense against the relevant prosecution.

The federal government had managed to devise a system in which obtaining the necessary documentation could effectively require a person to incriminate themselves.

Apparently, the constitutional principle of protection against self-incrimination had become another casualty of humanity's terrifying encounter with a shrub.

Congress subsequently replaced the old taxation framework with the Controlled Substances Act of 1970, placing marijuana in Schedule I.

The paperwork changed. The persecution continued.

## The Birth of the Botanical Boogeyman

One of the most notorious ingredients in Harry Anslinger's propaganda campaign was the 1933 case of Victor Licata, a young Florida man who killed members of his family.

Anslinger repeatedly presented the tragedy as evidence of the horrors supposedly unleashed by cannabis consumption. Subsequent examination of the case revealed evidence of significant psychiatric difficulties predating the incident and no reliable foundation for establishing that cannabis caused it. Nevertheless, the story became part of the national campaign against cannabis, and the distinction between a documented tragedy and a scientifically established explanation for that tragedy disappeared somewhere between the newspaper office and the narcotics commissioner's desk.

That distinction would ordinarily be considered rather important.

Apparently, when you're manufacturing a public emergency, causation becomes an optional accessory.

Anslinger's 1937 article *Marijuana: Assassin of Youth*, written with Courtney Ryley Cooper, provided another remarkable specimen of the emerging genre. It assembled allegations of violence, insanity, moral deterioration, and social catastrophe into a narrative capable of terrifying parents who knew virtually nothing about cannabis beyond what publications of this sort were telling them.

Then there was *Reefer Madness*.

Originally titled *Tell Your Children*, the 1936 exploitation film portrayed cannabis consumption as a gateway to a collection of psychological and behavioral catastrophes that would have made the average cannabis consumer wonder whether the filmmakers had accidentally obtained their research materials from a completely different pharmacological universe.

The characters laugh maniacally, display exaggerated sexual behavior, lose their grip on reality, and descend into an escalating collection of disasters. The overall effect resembles the fever dream of a prohibitionist who had never encountered an actual cannabis consumer and was unwilling to permit that inconvenience to interfere with the script.

Had the filmmakers been portraying extreme reactions to a dissociative drug such as PCP, portions of the performance might have been somewhat less bewildering. As an instructional depiction of ordinary cannabis consumption, however, it has approximately the educational value of a documentary about marine biology narrated by a man who believes dolphins are underwater horses.

The film was not an official Federal Bureau of Narcotics production; its producer subsequently sought Anslinger's endorsement. But it remains an artifact of the commercial, moral, and cultural machinery flourishing around the government's campaign.

What makes these old materials particularly useful today is that they allow us to examine misinformation in its relatively unrefined state, before subsequent generations became more sophisticated at laundering political agendas through institutional language.

The stories were sensational. The evidence was frequently inadequate. The conclusions were enormous. The political consequences were real.

And once those conclusions had been repeated sufficiently often, they became part of the inherited cultural understanding of cannabis. Parents taught them to children. Teachers repeated them in classrooms. Religious leaders incorporated them into moral instruction. Police officers enforced laws founded upon them. Legislators strengthened penalties without reexamining the assumptions underlying their existence.

Eventually, generations of Americans inherited the conclusion without ever having encountered the original argument.

That is how propaganda becomes folklore.

And unlike Minerva's folklore, this particular variety has proven spectacularly injurious to the people consuming it.

## God's Little Agricultural Oversight

There is something particularly entertaining about the participation of certain religious prohibitionists in the campaign against cannabis.

People who professed a belief in the divine creation of the natural world became extraordinarily comfortable with declaring a portion of that creation inherently evil. Apparently, God had enjoyed a particularly productive week creating the universe, then experienced a momentary lapse in judgment somewhere in the botanical department.

Fortunately, a number of American bureaucrats were available to correct the oversight.

The irony becomes more interesting when we consider the historically important role that plants and plant-derived substances have occupied within religious, spiritual, and ceremonial traditions around the world. Cannabis itself has been associated with religious and ceremonial practices in numerous cultures. Wine, perhaps the most familiar psychoactive substance within Western religious tradition, occupies a sacramental role in Christianity.

The fermentation of grape juice into an intoxicating substance somehow acquired divine significance, while the consumption of a naturally occurring flower became evidence of moral deterioration.

One wonders whether the distinction was established by revelation or by whichever agricultural lobby happened to have the better relationship with the congregation.

I have no objection to a person's spiritual or religious convictions informing their own relationship with intoxicants. Indeed, a thoughtful consideration of our relationships with intoxicants, consciousness, attachment, pleasure, and responsibility can form an immensely valuable part of spiritual development.

The trouble begins when somebody else's spiritual convictions are translated into laws governing my relationship with the natural world.

Spirituality loses something rather fundamental when it acquires the power to arrest people.

## The Government's Own Evidence

In 1938, New York City Mayor Fiorello La Guardia commissioned an investigation into cannabis that eventually produced the 1944 report *The Marihuana Problem in the City of New York*, prepared by the New York Academy of Medicine.

The resulting examination challenged several of the sensational assertions underlying the national cannabis campaign. Its findings did not support the proposition that cannabis consumption inevitably led to violent criminal behavior, physical dependence of the sort associated with certain other drugs, or widespread moral deterioration.

Anslinger did not respond by expressing gratitude for the opportunity to refine federal policy according to improved scientific evidence.

He attacked the report.

Nearly three decades later, Congress established the National Commission on Marihuana and Drug Abuse, chaired by former Pennsylvania Governor Raymond P. Shafer. Its 1972 report, *Marihuana: A Signal of Misunderstanding*, concluded that criminalization of private possession for personal use was unjustified and recommended a substantial departure from existing prohibitionist policy.

President Richard Nixon's administration declined to embrace its central recommendations.

Consider what happened here. The federal government authorized an investigation into a public-policy question, assembled an officially recognized commission, received the resulting findings, and proceeded to disregard conclusions that contradicted the administration's preferred direction.

The problem was no longer merely that governments lacked adequate information.

The problem was that they had acquired information they did not particularly care for.

That pattern should concern every citizen, regardless of their personal relationship with cannabis. When an institution can commission research, disregard its conclusions, and continue imposing policies that those conclusions undermine, we have wandered some considerable distance from evidence-based government.

At that point, the continued existence of the policy deserves more investigation than the continued existence of the plant.

## Hemp for Victory, Unless Otherwise Instructed

The United States government's 1942 film *Hemp for Victory* may be one of the finest surviving pieces of accidental political satire ever produced.

Only five years after the Marihuana Tax Act, the federal government was encouraging American farmers to grow hemp because wartime disruption had threatened supplies of strategically important fibers. Hemp was once again recognized for its usefulness in producing rope, cordage, and other materials essential to the war effort.

The plant had not changed. The chemistry had not changed. Its relationship with Satan had apparently undergone considerable administrative revision.

Suddenly, growing hemp was patriotic.

One can only imagine the confusion experienced by a farmer attempting to maintain an accurate understanding of his moral obligations.

Had the Japanese surrendered six months earlier, would his field have become a threat to national security again? Was the Almighty issuing temporary agricultural exemptions? Did salvation depend upon whether the rope was being used by the United States Navy? Was a farmer's eternal soul to be evaluated according to the date stamped on his cultivation permit, or did the Department of Agriculture possess some previously undisclosed authority over the admission requirements for Heaven?

What the film inadvertently preserved was evidence of the intellectual absurdity of the entire arrangement. The same government could recognize cannabis as a strategic national resource and maintain a prohibitionist framework surrounding it.

The plant was dangerous or indispensable according to the immediate requirements of the institution describing it.

The additional historical irony is that America's experiment with national alcohol prohibition had ended in December 1933, less than four years before the enactment of the Marihuana Tax Act. We had just experienced a prolonged demonstration of the criminal markets, corruption, enforcement costs, and social consequences associated with prohibiting a widely consumed intoxicant.

Having learned that particular lesson at considerable expense, we immediately proceeded to ignore it.

Alcohol, meanwhile, returned to its position within respectable American commerce. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, excessive alcohol consumption was responsible for an estimated annual average of approximately 178,000 deaths in the United States during 2020 and 2021. These estimates encompass a range of acute and chronic alcohol-attributable causes, from injuries to diseases associated with prolonged consumption.

That is the federal government's own accounting of an intoxicant our society accommodates through ordinary commercial, medical, and cultural institutions.

I do not offer that figure as an argument for prohibiting alcohol. I offer it as evidence that our historical allocation of outrage has had remarkably little to do with an objective assessment of pharmacological danger.

We possess a demonstrated capacity to coexist with substances capable of producing extraordinary harm, provided those substances have acquired the appropriate cultural legitimacy.

Cannabis spent generations being denied that legitimacy by people who had manufactured its illegitimacy in the first place.

## The Billion-Dollar Crop

In February 1938, only months after the passage of the Marihuana Tax Act, *Popular Mechanics* published an article entitled *New Billion-Dollar Crop*.

The crop was hemp.

A billion dollars. With a B. In nineteen thirty-fucking-eight.

The article described the development of machinery capable of making hemp processing substantially more economical and forecast an enormous range of potential commercial applications. It discussed thousands of textile products and projected that cellulose recovered from hemp hurds could form the basis for more than 25,000 products.

Those numbers were contemporary industrial projections rather than an independently verified catalog of 25,000 commercially manufactured hemp products. What makes them important is that the agricultural and industrial potential of cannabis was receiving serious attention at precisely the moment federal policy was making the development of that potential increasingly difficult.

People knew.

They were discussing the machinery, the economics, the fibers, the cellulose, the agricultural opportunities, and the potential for replacing imported raw materials. They were publishing those discussions in mainstream technical magazines for ordinary Americans to read.

This was not knowledge we could only have acquired after another century of scientific advancement. It was knowledge already circulating among people who were actively contemplating the next generation of American industry.

And much of the industrial potential discussed in those publications remained undeveloped for decades.

How much more evidence of a historical interruption do we require before we become genuinely curious about the interests that helped facilitate it?

## The Road We Might Have Taken

In 1941, Henry Ford unveiled an experimental automobile whose lightweight plastic body panels were mounted on a tubular steel frame. The automobile is generally known as the Soybean Car, although historical descriptions also associate its construction with flax, hemp, wheat, ramie, and other agricultural fibers.

Contemporary reports and the recollections of people involved in its development establish the agricultural-materials character of the project, although the original formulation of its panels has not survived in a form that allows every ingredient and proportion to be definitively reconstructed. Some accounts emphasized soybean fiber in a phenolic resin matrix; others described more varied combinations of plant fibers.

The underlying industrial vision, however, could hardly be clearer.

Ford had spent years investigating ways to connect agriculture with manufacturing. His chemurgic ambitions envisioned a world in which agricultural products supplied raw materials for industrial processes, creating new opportunities for farmers and manufacturers alike.

The significance of this experiment becomes more apparent when considered alongside the subsequent history of petroleum-derived plastics, which we examined in *Plastic Fantastic*.

Both industrial pathways offered enormous potential. One of them received generations of investment, infrastructure development, scientific inquiry, engineering, commercial expansion, and political support. The other was permitted to develop in fragments, particularly where its constituent crops escaped the regulatory machinery imposed upon cannabis.

Imagine where we might be today if those pathways had been allowed to mature alongside one another.

Imagine nearly a century of sustained research into agricultural cellulose, natural fibers, vegetable oils, lignin, plant-derived polymers, biological feedstocks, fungal materials, and the extraordinary possibilities available through their combination. Consider what might have emerged from the collaboration of agricultural scientists, mechanical engineers, chemists, materials specialists, farmers, foresters, and manufacturers working across those disciplines.

Modern industrial chemistry has demonstrated numerous ways of using biomass and plant-derived compounds to produce materials traditionally manufactured from petroleum. Renewable feedstocks can be transformed into solvents, lubricants, fuels, polymers, coatings, adhesives, and chemical intermediates. The processing requirements, economic viability, and environmental consequences vary considerably according to the application.

But the fundamental technological opportunity is real.

We can grow portions of our industrial feedstock.

We can recover useful agricultural materials from waste streams.

We can manufacture certain products using biological processes.

We can cultivate fungi that bind agricultural residues into useful structural and packaging materials.

We can develop polymer matrices from renewable feedstocks and reinforce them with fibers grown on farms.

None of those possibilities requires us to pretend that every petroleum-derived material has an immediately available agricultural substitute. They require us to recognize that the industrial landscape we inherited represents the cumulative results of particular historical decisions, investments, political arrangements, and research priorities.

It is one landscape among numerous landscapes humanity might have developed.

## What Was Taken From Us

The damage associated with hemp's marginalization cannot be measured simply in acres that went unplanted.

Consider the farmer who might have diversified into fiber production. Consider the machine shop that might have developed increasingly sophisticated harvesting and processing equipment, the textile manufacturer that might have perfected new methods of working with bast fibers, the regional processing facility that might have supplied thousands of local farms, and the materials laboratory that might have spent several generations refining agricultural composites.

Consider the businesses those developments would have supported: equipment manufacturers, maintenance specialists, seed breeders, transportation companies, agricultural consultants, independent laboratories, mill operators, construction firms, and a multitude of enterprises whose existence would have depended upon the emergence of an industrial hemp ecosystem.

There are no employment statistics for the people those businesses never hired. There are no economic development reports documenting communities whose industries were never established. There are no market forecasts for technologies that were never developed because the foundational industries upon which they depended were denied the opportunity to mature.

And perhaps most importantly, there is no comprehensive inventory of the knowledge we failed to accumulate.

Knowledge compounds. One discovery becomes the foundation of another. An engineer improves a machine, a farmer discovers how to grow a crop better suited to it, a processor develops a new method of handling the resulting material, and a manufacturer discovers a use nobody had contemplated when the original research began.

A functioning industrial ecosystem is a continuous conversation among thousands of people, each building upon the experiences of others.

Interrupt that conversation for several generations and you do not merely lose the products that might have been manufactured during those years. You lose the subsequent generations of products that would have emerged from the knowledge accumulated in manufacturing them.

What would hemp-based polymer compounds look like today after eighty years of uninterrupted industrial research? What might we understand about natural fiber reinforcement, recyclable agricultural composites, biodegradable binding systems, plant-derived lubricants, or industrial oils? How far might hemp-based construction have progressed? What opportunities might have emerged from integrating cannaculture and mycoculture within regional agricultural and manufacturing economies?

Imagine combining hemp fiber, agricultural hurd, fungal mycelium, plant-derived resins, and cellulose-based materials within production systems designed from their inception around renewability and end-of-life recovery.

Some of these technologies already exist in relatively early commercial forms. Others are being actively investigated. Many of the engineers and entrepreneurs working in those fields are discovering that their greatest difficulties lie not in identifying useful properties within the raw materials, but in establishing the surrounding agricultural, economic, manufacturing, and regulatory infrastructure necessary to use them at scale.

They are building the infrastructure we should have spent the last century building.

That is the historical bill I would like to present to Harry Anslinger, the members of Congress who facilitated his ambitions, the newspaper proprietors who manufactured the accompanying hysteria, and the institutions whose beneficiaries have spent generations accumulating the rewards of a radically different industrial trajectory.

Unfortunately, most of the original recipients are unavailable to accept delivery.

The debt, however, remains ours.

## The Useful Little Bastard

Hemp is a remarkably versatile agricultural crop, and understanding its industrial potential requires looking at the entire plant rather than concentrating exclusively upon the chemical characteristics of its flowers.

The outer bast fibers of the stalk possess properties useful in textiles, cordage, paper production, insulation, and reinforcement within composite materials. The inner woody portion, called hurd or shiv, has applications in construction, animal bedding, absorbents, engineered boards, cellulose production, and various manufactured materials. The seeds provide edible oils, protein, and other nutritional components, while their oils offer opportunities extending beyond food into cosmetics, coatings, lubricants, and potentially fuel applications.

Hempseed oil is rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids and can be incorporated into a broad range of food formulations. Hemp protein ingredients can be used in foods intended to supplement dietary protein. Seed-derived materials offer opportunities for experimentation in food manufacturing, animal nutrition where approved and supported, and specialty chemical production.

The plant's industrial versatility becomes more interesting when we consider the potential for using several output streams from the same agricultural system. A processing facility producing fiber might also generate substantial quantities of hurd. Seed processing produces oil and protein-rich fractions. The economic viability of an agricultural enterprise can improve substantially when materials once considered waste become valuable inputs to other enterprises.

The agricultural questions surrounding these possibilities are fascinating. Different cultivars are suited to different purposes. Crop density, stem diameter, fiber quality, seed production, climate, soil conditions, harvesting techniques, retting, and mechanical processing all influence the value of the resulting materials.

This is the stuff that agricultural students should be investigating through FFA, 4-H, university extension programs, and agricultural research institutions.

Instead, generations of agricultural students inherited a cultural framework within which simply expressing an interest in cannabis could generate unnecessary suspicion.

It should have been entirely normal for a teenager interested in materials engineering to develop a school project examining hemp fiber reinforcement. A student interested in nutrition might investigate hempseed proteins. A mechanically inclined student might develop a more efficient fiber separator. A young farmer might experiment with locally adapted cultivars and rotational practices.

The future of an agricultural industry begins in places like these.

How many opportunities disappeared because the subject of the student's curiosity was culturally unacceptable?

## Paul Benhaim and the Road Back

There are people working today to reconstruct portions of the industrial landscape we abandoned.

Paul Benhaim, a British-born entrepreneur whose work has extended across Australia and the international hemp industry, has spent decades developing hemp food products, advocating for policy reform, establishing businesses, and pursuing the industrial applications of cannabis.

His work with Hemp Foods Australia contributed to a long campaign for the recognition of hempseed products as legitimate foods in Australia. He has also participated in the development of The Hemp Plastic Company, of which he is chairman, bringing agricultural fibers into the existing polymer manufacturing ecosystem.

The company develops hemp-containing compounds that can be supplied in pellet form and processed using established industrial equipment. Depending upon the formulation, these materials incorporate hemp-derived components into polymer matrices suited to injection molding and other manufacturing processes.

This represents an important technological bridge. A manufacturer does not necessarily need to abandon its existing equipment and construct an entirely new factory to begin incorporating agricultural materials into its products. By developing compounds compatible with established manufacturing processes, companies like Benhaim's create practical opportunities for reducing the proportion of virgin fossil-derived material in particular applications.

A hemp-reinforced petroleum-based polymer does not automatically become biodegradable because it contains hemp. Its environmental properties depend upon the complete formulation, its use, its durability, and what happens to it afterward. That is precisely why continued materials research is so valuable.

The industrial opportunities are being explored in real manufacturing environments, with real machinery, real product specifications, and real economic constraints.

In April 2026, Benhaim appeared before an Australian Senate inquiry examining opportunities for the development of Australia's hemp industry. The inquiry considered a broad range of questions involving agricultural production, processing capacity, regulation, manufacturing, export markets, and industrial development.

The existence of that inquiry is itself an interesting historical marker. Nearly ninety years after *Popular Mechanics* discussed hemp as a potential billion-dollar crop, governments are still examining how to permit the agricultural and industrial development of a plant whose basic legitimacy should have been settled generations ago.

Benhaim and his contemporaries are doing valuable work.

They are also doing work that should never have been interrupted.

## From Seed to Finished Product

Equally interesting developments are taking place within China's industrial hemp sector, where researchers, agricultural specialists, machinery manufacturers, and textile producers are working across the entire production chain.

The work associated with Xue Rong Long and the Zhonglong hemp and kenaf enterprise offers a particularly useful glimpse into the complexity of that chain.

Producing useful textile fiber begins long before a stalk arrives at a decortication machine. Seed genetics influence agricultural characteristics. Cultivation practices influence stem thickness, branching, uniformity, and fiber quality. Harvesting methods affect the material presented to subsequent processing systems. Retting, mechanical separation, cleaning, carding, softening, and spinning must all be coordinated with the requirements of the intended finished product.

A problem encountered at the yarn-production stage may originate in a decision made before the seed was planted.

The same principle applies across other industrial applications. A manufacturer producing composite reinforcement may require different fiber characteristics from a textile manufacturer. A hemp-lime construction application may require a carefully specified hurd. A food manufacturer may require particular seed characteristics. Processing machinery has to accommodate those differences, and the surrounding agricultural economy has to support their development.

This is sophisticated, interdisciplinary industrial work.

It is also gloriously ordinary.

There is something immensely satisfying about watching people investigate crop genetics, processing machinery, material strength, fiber characteristics, and manufacturing economics without having to conduct their discussions beneath an enormous moral question mark hanging over the botanical identity of their raw material.

The plant is finally being permitted to be useful without first being required to explain itself.

## Minerva Meets the Laboratory

Meanwhile, the medical descendants of Minerva Mayhem McCoy are finally getting some rather sophisticated equipment.

Modern cannabinoid research is becoming considerably more interesting than the familiar arguments concerning whether cannabis can alleviate chemotherapy-induced nausea or improve appetite. Researchers are examining specific cannabinoids, whole-plant extracts, combinations of compounds, pharmaceutical formulations, differences in administration, physiological signaling mechanisms, and applications that would have been difficult even to contemplate before the discovery of the endocannabinoid system.

In 2025, *Nature Medicine* published the results of a phase III randomized, placebo-controlled trial involving a standardized full-spectrum cannabis extract, VER-01, for chronic lower-back pain. Among the 820 participants, 54.1 percent of those receiving the extract achieved at least a 30 percent reduction in pain, compared with 39.5 percent of participants receiving placebo.

The difference was statistically significant. The study also examined measures involving physical functioning, sleep, and other patient-reported outcomes, providing information about the treatment beyond the simple question of whether patients experienced less pain.

A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial published in *Headache* in 2026 investigated vaporized cannabis preparations for the treatment of acute migraine. A combination of THC and CBD produced significantly greater pain relief and pain freedom at two hours than placebo.

A systematic review published in September 2026 examined randomized clinical evidence concerning cannabinoid-based interventions for lower-back pain and migraine, identifying moderate-certainty evidence supporting particular formulations and applications.

These are not insignificant developments. Chronic pain and migraine affect enormous numbers of people, often interfering with employment, family life, sleep, mobility, and the ordinary enjoyment of existence.

Researchers are also exploring the properties of cannabinoids beyond THC and CBD. Cannabigerol, cannabinol, cannabichromene, tetrahydrocannabinolic acid, cannabidiolic acid, and numerous other compounds represent an expansive research landscape. Their individual properties, interactions, pharmacological activity, and potential applications remain subjects of ongoing investigation.

Some scientists are particularly interested in the possibility that combinations of cannabis-derived compounds may produce effects differing from those of isolated constituents. The proposed entourage effect encompasses several related hypotheses concerning these interactions. Establishing which combinations produce clinically meaningful benefits, under what conditions, and through which mechanisms will require considerable additional research.

The interesting thing is that this question brings modern science back toward an observation long familiar to practitioners of traditional medicine: the therapeutic behavior of a complex preparation may not be adequately described by examining one isolated ingredient.

That possibility should inspire experimental curiosity rather than ideological indigestion.

Cannabinoid-related oncology research offers another particularly compelling example. The ARISTOCRAT phase II clinical trial was designed to investigate a cannabinoid formulation, nabiximols, alongside conventional chemotherapy in patients with recurrent glioblastoma.

Whether that particular intervention ultimately demonstrates meaningful improvements in clinical outcomes is a question for the research itself. Its existence establishes that serious investigators consider cannabinoid-related oncology applications worthy of rigorous examination.

I find myself wondering what Minerva would make of all this.

I suspect she would be fascinated by the instruments, rather amused by the terminology, and particularly interested in what the researchers discovered about the plants she had spent a lifetime observing.

She might also have a few questions for the scientists.

Good scientists would probably find those questions worth hearing.

## The Difference Between Consumption and Addiction

One of the more enduring consequences of prohibitionist mythology has been our tendency to approach cannabis consumption as though it were inherently symptomatic of something.

People who drink wine are often presumed to enjoy wine. People who drink coffee are presumed to enjoy coffee. People who take up woodworking are presumed to enjoy woodworking, although the price of certain power tools raises legitimate questions concerning their relationship with discretionary spending.

People who consume cannabis, however, have historically been expected to establish why they do so, preferably by presenting either an acceptable medical explanation or an appropriately apologetic account of their recreational preferences.

I reject the premise.

The enjoyment of cannabis is itself a legitimate reason for consuming cannabis.

Human beings have spent millennia exploring altered states of consciousness through music, dance, meditation, religious ceremonies, physical exertion, fasting, sensory experiences, and an extraordinarily diverse collection of naturally occurring and manufactured substances. We alter our consciousness constantly, deliberately, and often quite productively.

Some people find that cannabis helps them relax. Others find it conducive to introspection, creative exploration, sensory appreciation, social interaction, meditation, or the enjoyment of music. Others use it to address symptoms or improve aspects of their daily lives.

Some simply enjoy the experience.

Why should that require an elaborate medical defense?

Cannabis can produce impairment, interact with other substances, and contribute to problematic patterns of consumption. These are legitimate subjects for investigation and education. They are also quite different from the proposition that consumption is inherently pathological.

Addiction and problematic consumption should be understood in the context of the individual's behavior, circumstances, health, and relationship with the substance. The fact that somebody has maintained a relationship with cannabis for forty years reveals remarkably little about whether that relationship has been destructive.

The important information concerns the character of the relationship, not merely its duration.

One of the great injuries inflicted by prohibitionist propaganda has been the corrosion of trust necessary for honest conversations about these subjects. When institutions spend generations exaggerating dangers, disseminating questionable information, and punishing people whose experience contradicts the prescribed narrative, they damage their own credibility.

When those institutions eventually identify a genuine concern, they discover that the people they spent decades lying to are understandably reluctant to accept their latest assurances of expertise.

Propaganda does not merely distort the subject of the propaganda.

It damages our ability to communicate truthfully about that subject afterward.

## When the Cops Started Asking Questions

One of the more encouraging chapters in the history of drug-policy reform begins with people who had spent their professional lives enforcing the very laws they would eventually work to dismantle.

Jack A. Cole served for twenty-six years with the New Jersey State Police, including twelve years working undercover in narcotics enforcement. He had experienced the machinery of America's war on drugs from a perspective unavailable to most of its political architects and enthusiastic supporters.

He had been one of the people required to make the policies happen.

In 2002, Cole joined retired Captain Peter Christ, Detective Howard Wooldridge, Detective Daniel Solano, and Constable John Gayder in founding Law Enforcement Against Prohibition, better known as LEAP.

The organization brought together law enforcement professionals and others with criminal justice experience who had come to question the effectiveness and consequences of drug prohibition.

Former Seattle Police Chief Norm Stamper became another prominent advocate associated with LEAP. Stamper had spent thirty-four years in policing, including six years leading Seattle's police department. His subsequent work challenged numerous assumptions surrounding American law enforcement and drug policy.

These were people who understood the difference between the theoretical objectives of a law and the consequences encountered by the people enforcing it.

They had witnessed the resources devoted to pursuing prohibited substances, the criminal markets sustained by prohibition, the injuries inflicted upon communities, and the institutional contradictions arising from policies that treated complex social and health questions primarily as criminal justice problems.

Cole and the other founders of LEAP demonstrated something I consider particularly important: the capacity of human beings to examine the institutions they have served, acknowledge the consequences of their participation, and change their positions in response to what they have learned.

There is courage in that.

It is considerably easier to defend a career than to examine it. It is easier to preserve one's professional mythology than to question the institutional arrangements that provided one's authority, identity, and livelihood.

LEAP, subsequently renamed the Law Enforcement Action Partnership, expanded its work into broader questions involving policing, incarceration, criminal justice reform, and relationships between law enforcement institutions and the communities they serve.

Its origins remain a particularly important part of the story of cannabis prohibition because they undermine the assumption that opposition to prohibition must originate with people hostile to law enforcement.

Some of the most compelling arguments against the drug war have come from the people who fought it.

And the damage they recognized was substantial.

A 2020 report by the American Civil Liberties Union examining marijuana enforcement between 2010 and 2018 documented approximately 6.1 million marijuana-possession arrests during that period. It found that Black Americans were 3.64 times as likely as white Americans to be arrested for marijuana possession, despite broadly comparable rates of cannabis use.

These were not statistics from the 1930s.

They were statistics from a supposedly enlightened twenty-first-century America, a country already celebrating its gradual emergence from cannabis prohibition.

Six million encounters with the criminal justice system over possession of a plant.

Six million opportunities to interfere with employment, education, housing, families, financial security, dignity, and the lives of people whose behavior frequently involved no injury to anybody.

Jack Cole and the other professionals who established LEAP recognized that enforcing such policies was not equivalent to protecting the public.

In many cases, the enforcement itself had become the injury.

## Prohibition Gets a Business Degree

We should be particularly skeptical of the tendency to describe the contemporary cannabis landscape as legalized.

Legal for whom? Under what conditions? At what expense? In which jurisdiction? And subject to whose continuing permission?

The cultivation of industrial hemp was federally removed from the marijuana definition under the Controlled Substances Act through the 2018 Farm Bill, provided it met the statutory definition of hemp. That definition established a delta-9 THC concentration threshold of 0.3 percent on a dry-weight basis.

Farmers were subsequently required to navigate a regulatory system involving approved production programs, licensing, sampling, testing, reporting, and procedures for handling crops determined to exceed permissible thresholds.

Anyone familiar with agricultural production understands that plants are not manufactured to the dimensional tolerances of precision-machined automotive components. Genetics interact with environmental conditions. Individual plants vary. Chemical concentrations change as plants mature. Sampling and laboratory measurements possess limitations and uncertainties.

Yet the farmer's relationship with this crop can be dramatically altered by a numerical determination concerning its chemistry.

A field of plants intended for legitimate agricultural purposes can become a regulatory problem because those plants have failed to arrange their molecular affairs according to congressional specifications.

Some growers and manufacturers working with higher-THC cannabis face additional layers of licensing fees, surveillance requirements, restricted access to financial services, specialized security obligations, inventory tracking, transportation restrictions, and jurisdictional complications.

These requirements differ considerably across states and types of operation, but the cumulative consequence is often the same: participation in the industry becomes expensive.

What kind of businesses are generally best positioned to absorb large licensing costs, extensive administrative obligations, and significant legal uncertainty? Businesses with substantial capital. What kind of businesses struggle to survive under those conditions? Independent growers, smaller manufacturers, family-owned operations, and people attempting to enter an emerging industry without the financial backing of wealthy investors.

The traditional regulatory approach has an unfortunate habit of converting public permission into a commercially valuable asset that can be acquired disproportionately by those with the financial resources to obtain it.

A plant once criminalized becomes the foundation of a restricted commercial marketplace, and many of the people whose knowledge and advocacy helped make that marketplace possible discover that they cannot afford admission.

That arrangement deserves considerably more skepticism than it receives.

The latest developments in federal cannabis policy illustrate the problem.

In April 2026, the federal government placed certain FDA-approved marijuana products and marijuana covered by qualifying state medical-marijuana licenses into Schedule III of the Controlled Substances Act. It also established a specialized federal registration pathway for eligible medical-marijuana businesses. Broader marijuana rescheduling remained subject to separate federal proceedings.

This was not cannabis descheduling. It did not establish unrestricted interstate commerce, eliminate federal controls, or provide adult-use cannabis with equivalent federal recognition.

Meanwhile, legislation enacted in November 2025 narrowed the federal definition of hemp. Among its provisions is a limit of 0.4 milligrams of total THC and similar-effect cannabinoids per container of certain finished hemp-derived cannabinoid products. Most of the new definition's provisions are currently scheduled to take effect on December 11, 2026, following a legislative delay.

Under that framework, products previously falling within the federal definition of hemp may find themselves reclassified and subject to marijuana controls.

One part of the government is gradually reducing certain restrictions upon medical marijuana while another is preparing to intensify restrictions upon portions of the hemp industry.

The left hand appears to be attempting to remove the right hand's shackles while the right hand is busy purchasing additional shackles at public expense.

## Follow the Money, Again

The commercial interests surrounding modern cannabis restrictions are not particularly difficult to find.

In 2025, several alcohol-industry trade organizations supported congressional efforts to prohibit or substantially restrict intoxicating hemp-derived products. Other organizations within the alcohol distribution industry opposed the proposed restrictions, recognizing both the commercial opportunities presented by cannabis-derived beverages and the consequences of eliminating a developing market.

In October 2026, reporting by *Politico* described communications between representatives of major alcohol companies and state attorneys general concerning efforts to preserve the forthcoming federal restrictions on hemp-derived cannabinoid products.

We therefore have contemporary documentation of commercial interests attempting to influence government policy concerning an emerging competitor.

Sound familiar?

The alcohol industry's positions are not uniform. Some businesses want to participate in the cannabis beverage market. Others favor regulatory arrangements modeled after their own industry. Others have supported substantial restrictions upon the existing hemp-derived beverage marketplace.

These positions should be examined individually, including the economic interests behind them.

What I find particularly interesting is the ease with which commercial concerns can be translated into declarations of public safety.

A competing product presents a challenge to an established industry. The established industry possesses political relationships, lobbying resources, and a substantial interest in the regulatory environment governing the new product. Questions of public safety are raised, restrictions proposed, and political support cultivated.

How convenient that the resulting public-safety measures may also restrict competition.

Perhaps every participant is motivated exclusively by an overwhelming concern for the welfare of humanity. Perhaps the shareholders, executives, lobbyists, and industry associations involved have entirely forgotten the commercial implications of the policies they are advocating.

I am prepared to entertain that possibility for approximately as long as it takes to swallow my beer.

The appropriate public response is to examine the evidence offered in support of any proposed restriction, the proportionality of the proposed response, the individuals and organizations advocating it, the distribution of its benefits, and the people expected to bear its costs.

We have approximately ninety years of historical experience demonstrating what can happen when we neglect those questions.

I would prefer that we not spend the next ninety learning the same fucking lesson.

## What Freedom Would Actually Look Like

I want to see cannabis entirely removed from the Controlled Substances Act.

All of it.

Industrial hemp should be treated as an agricultural crop. Its industrial applications should be governed by the ordinary requirements applicable to the products manufactured from it. Its nutritional applications should be examined under the same scientific and food-safety principles applicable to comparable foods. Its medicinal applications should be investigated according to the appropriate standards of scientific inquiry and informed patient care.

Cannabis intended for personal consumption should cease to be treated as an inherently suspect category of human behavior.

Existing laws addressing dangerous conduct, fraud, adulteration, environmental damage, impaired driving, and other demonstrable injuries already provide numerous tools for responding to actual problems. Where those tools require refinement, we can discuss their refinement according to the problem being addressed rather than the mythology surrounding a particular plant.

The justification for restricting human behavior should arise from the consequences of that behavior, not from an inherited institutional suspicion of the object involved.

I want farmers to be able to grow cannabis without the cultural and administrative residue of the narcotics industry attached to their agricultural operations. I want researchers to be able to investigate cannabis without their curiosity being subjected to extraordinary political interference. I want independent manufacturers to develop useful products without first acquiring permission from a regulatory system whose costs disproportionately favor large concentrations of capital.

I want people like Minerva to be able to preserve, develop, and share their knowledge within their communities without carrying the historical stigma of criminality.

And I want individuals to possess the authority to explore their own relationships with consciousness without being expected to explain themselves to institutions whose collective historical record on that subject is frankly embarrassing.

This will require more of us than merely rewriting a collection of laws. It will require developing a culture sufficiently mature to discuss responsibility, autonomy, health, pleasure, dependency, and harm without immediately reaching for prohibition as a substitute for understanding.

That is work worth doing.

If we expect humanity to coexist peacefully and productively with increasingly capable artificial intelligence, we should probably establish some reasonably dependable foundations of human intelligence first.

A civilization that still requires extraordinary bureaucratic intervention to manage the existence of a flowering plant might want to devote a little additional attention to its own intellectual development before appointing itself the responsible steward of machine intelligence.

Just a thought.

## A Plant Again

There is something wonderfully peaceful about the thought of a future in which cannabis has become thoroughly ordinary.

A farmer grows industrial hemp. An agricultural student experiments with fiber crops. An engineer develops a better decorticator. A manufacturer investigates renewable composites. A physician explores emerging cannabinoid therapies with a patient. Researchers examine the countless questions that remain unanswered concerning the plant and the physiology with which its compounds interact.

Someone enjoys cannabis recreationally. Someone else has no particular interest in it. Someone exploring their consciousness discovers something meaningful about themselves. Somebody struggling with a destructive pattern of consumption finds assistance from people who understand the difference between compassion and punishment.

Minerva makes a salve.

The world somehow continues rotating.

What I want before I leave this planet is considerably larger than the liberation of cannabis. I want to see America develop into a culture that has earned the right to describe itself as great, a culture capable of examining its history without becoming defensive, recognizing its mistakes without becoming paralyzed by embarrassment, and allowing its citizens to develop the knowledge and judgment necessary to govern their own lives.

Perhaps by the time we accomplish that, the distinction between an American culture and an Earthling culture will have become rather less interesting to us. Perhaps we will have matured sufficiently to recognize that the natural world, human knowledge, and the extraordinary possibilities of our existence belong to something considerably larger than the political boundaries within which we happened to be born.

Perhaps we'll just call ourselves Milky Waylings.

I suspect Waylon Jennings would have gotten a kick out of that.

In the meantime, we have serious work to do. We have an agricultural inheritance to recover, scientific questions to investigate, industries to develop, historical narratives to reexamine, and generations of accumulated bullshit to shovel out of the way.

We need not feel embarrassed that previous generations believed what they were told about cannabis. They were subjected to a concerted campaign of misinformation presented through institutions they had been encouraged to trust. Physicians, farmers, legislators, teachers, parents, and ordinary citizens inherited portions of a story that had been manufactured around them.

We should, however, become considerably less tolerant of continuing to perpetuate that story now that we have access to the evidence necessary to dismantle it.

The official narrative has enjoyed nearly a century of undeserved authority. Let it retire from its position as canon, become a cannonball, and allow us the satisfaction of launching the fucking thing beyond the gravitational influence of the planet.

There is an enormous amount left to learn about cannabis. That should excite us. We have thousands of years of inherited observations, rapidly developing scientific capabilities, an extraordinary diversity of biological compounds, and a growing collection of agricultural and industrial technologies whose ultimate potential we have barely begun to explore.

The only cannabis-related panic worth preserving is the one we experience whenever somebody begins suggesting that curiosity, cultivation, knowledge, and responsible enjoyment should once again require their permission.

We knew better than to allow its prohibition nearly a century ago. We simply didn't know we knew. Now, as we gradually emerge from the intellectual coma into which we allowed ourselves to be induced, we have the opportunity to recover something considerably more valuable than a plant.

We have the opportunity to recover our curiosity, our judgment, and our authority over our own existence.

The plant, throughout all of this, remained a plant.

It was the human beings who lost their fucking minds.
