## The Carpenter and the Hammer

I have no idea whether Jesus ever swore.

Neither do you.

Neither does your pastor.

Neither does the guy on YouTube with the twelve-part documentary, the dramatic thunder sound effects, and the beard trimmed so aggressively it appears to have received legal counsel.

Historically speaking, we simply do not know.

Part of the problem is that profanity is culturally slippery. What scandalizes one era becomes ordinary in another. What offends in one language may barely register in the next. Ancient Aramaic does not pour neatly into modern English, and there is no surviving gospel account of a disciple writing, “Then the Lord struck His thumb with a hammer and uttered a phrase unfit for synagogue use.”

Still, I admit I find the image useful.

A young carpenter misses a nail, crushes a thumb, and lets fly with something colorful. Joseph looks up from the other side of the shop and says, “Language.”

Jesus replies, “Dad, have you ever tried building a table with Roman nails?”

Mary immediately volunteers for permanent duty at the soapmaking station.

The image is absurd.

It is also revealing.

Many people find the idea uncomfortable because we often imagine holy people as sanitized people. We picture goodness speaking in carefully edited greeting cards, floating through life with soft lighting, perfect posture, and the emotional range of a decorative candle.

But the Jesus of the New Testament was not inoffensively bland. He was compassionate, forgiving, and committed to mercy. He was also willing to flip tables, shame hypocrites in public, and compare respected religious authorities to whitewashed tombs.

Whatever else one believes about him, “inoffensive beige spiritual wallpaper” does not appear to have been the brand.

That matters because it points toward a larger problem.

Why are we so frightened by the possibility that a good person might occasionally use a bad word, yet so easily fooled by bad people who know all the right words?

## Whitewashed Tombs and Other Polite Disasters

Human beings love moral shortcuts.

We use them because character is inconvenient. Determining whether someone is honest, brave, generous, compassionate, reliable, or wise takes time. It requires observation. It requires context. It requires watching what people do when there is something to lose.

Words are easier.

Words can be counted, categorized, approved, condemned, censored, rewarded, punished, and mistaken for evidence of virtue by anyone with a checklist and enough fluorescent lighting to kill a fern.

This is why societies repeatedly drift toward policing symbols instead of cultivating character. Symbols stand still. Character keeps slipping out the back door with a fake mustache and a consulting contract.

A person can learn the approved vocabulary in an afternoon.

Becoming a decent human being usually takes considerably longer.

That confusion is not new. One of the sharper edges in the New Testament is aimed directly at people who had mastered the appearance of righteousness while missing the point of righteousness entirely. Jesus was not especially gentle with that crowd. He called them whitewashed tombs.

It is a brutal image.

A tomb can look beautiful from the outside. It can be polished, decorated, admired, and placed prominently where everyone can see how respectable it is. But underneath the paint, it remains full of death.

That metaphor has survived for two thousand years because human beings keep needing it.

Every age invents new moral cosmetics. Sometimes it is etiquette. Sometimes it is religious correctness. Sometimes it is political language. Sometimes it is corporate sensitivity theater. Sometimes it is a carefully curated public identity with the depth of a souvenir spoon and the aggression of a small dog in a sweater.

The details change.

The temptation does not.

People learn how to display goodness long before they learn how to become good.

That does not mean manners are worthless. Courtesy matters. Context matters. Consideration matters. Language can wound, degrade, intimidate, humiliate, manipulate, and divide. Anyone who has spent more than ten minutes among human beings understands that words have power.

But words are not character.

That distinction matters because some of the most dishonest people you will ever meet have impeccable manners. Some of the most exploitative executives in the building will never utter a profanity. Some of the most manipulative politicians in history spoke with perfect decorum while sharpening knives under the table.

The damage they caused had nothing to do with vocabulary.

It had everything to do with character.

## The Man Who Never Swore

Most of us have met him.

Maybe not literally, but certainly in spirit.

He is the man who always knows the correct thing to say. His language is clean. His manners are polished. His public image has been maintained for so long that even he may no longer know where the performance ends and the person begins.

He would never use an offensive word in mixed company.

He would never violate a social norm in a way that might draw attention.

He knows which phrases are safe, which opinions are acceptable, and which signals communicate virtue to the audience currently holding the clipboard.

In many environments, he thrives.

People trust him.

They promote him.

They admire him.

Sometimes they follow him straight into the ditch.

The problem is that none of these qualities tell us very much about his character. He may be honest or dishonest. Generous or selfish. Courageous or cowardly. Genuinely decent or merely fluent in the local dialect of appearing decent.

Language alone tells us very little.

That should be obvious, but entire institutions behave as if it were not. Schools, churches, corporations, political movements, nonprofits, social platforms, and governments all tend to prefer what can be seen, measured, documented, and enforced. They are often better at identifying a forbidden word than recognizing a rotten soul.

This is not always malicious. Institutions need rules. Communities need norms. Families need boundaries. A workplace without standards becomes a swamp with payroll software. Civilization does require some basic agreement about how people should behave around one another.

But rules can only do so much.

Rules can prohibit behavior.

They cannot manufacture virtue.

Rules can encourage civility.

They cannot create compassion.

Rules can punish violations.

They cannot produce wisdom.

A church can discourage profanity without producing mercy. A company can enforce professional language without creating respect. A school can regulate speech without teaching kindness. A government can pass laws until the shelves groan like overfed donkeys and still discover that integrity refuses to arrive on command.

This is the ancient problem wearing modern shoes.

Compliance is easier than character.

Etiquette is easier than conscience.

Polish is easier than virtue.

And because polish is visible, we keep mistaking it for proof.

## The Bastard Who Saved Your Life

Then there is the other person.

The bastard who saved your life.

He may be a mechanic, logger, lineman, fisherman, carpenter, rancher, soldier, bartender, nurse, welder, volunteer firefighter, or the neighbor who somehow owns three generators, two chainsaws, a suspiciously organized garage, and enough extension cords to power a small republic.

His vocabulary would horrify a church committee.

Her social media posts would probably trigger an HR review.

Neither one is likely to be invited to deliver a keynote address on civility, unless the conference organizers have lost a bet or found courage in the glovebox.

And yet when life comes apart at the seams, these are often the people everyone hopes will answer the phone.

They show up.

Not because they are saints.

Not because they are polished.

Not because they have memorized the preferred ceremonial language of the educated class.

They show up because they understand responsibility.

A funny thing happens when real trouble arrives. The standards by which we judge people change very quickly. The executive who curated every sentence becomes less interesting than the electrician who can restore power. The person who always says the right thing becomes less valuable than the person willing to do the difficult thing. Entire categories of social performance evaporate the moment reality starts demanding competence.

A flood does not care about your preferred terminology.

A wildfire does not pause to evaluate your etiquette.

A broken axle in the middle of nowhere remains stubbornly indifferent to your mastery of approved language.

Reality is a ruthless editor.

It cuts branding, messaging, posturing, politeness theater, résumé fog, and carefully varnished appearances from the manuscript. What remains is usually simple.

Did you show up?

Did you tell the truth?

Did you help?

Did you keep your word?

Did you do the hard thing when the easy thing was available and nobody was filming?

That is where character earns its keep.

Many people with rough edges pass that test beautifully. Some of the kindest people you will ever meet can make a sailor blush. Some of the most generous people alive can string together profanity with the verbal architecture of a Gothic cathedral and the emotional precision of a thrown brick.

This does not make profanity virtuous.

It makes it human.

People swear for many reasons. Anger. Pain. Fear. Emphasis. Humor. Bonding. Surprise. Grief. Affection. Creativity. A deep and prayerful encounter with plumbing.

Modern research has even complicated the lazy assumption that profanity comes from stupidity or a limited vocabulary. Some studies have found links between swearing and verbal fluency, emotional expression, pain tolerance, and authenticity. That does not mean every foul-mouthed mechanic is Aristotle with a socket wrench. It simply means reality is more interesting than the rulebook.

Language is a tool.

Like any tool, it can be used well or badly.

A hammer can build a house or ruin a thumb. A word can wound or relieve pressure. Profanity can be cruel, lazy, funny, expressive, affectionate, childish, accurate, obnoxious, poetic, or exactly what the situation ordered because “goodness gracious” was not structurally rated for the load.

The question is not whether the word is clean.

The question is what the person is building with it.

## What Kind of Goodness Are We Trying to Build?

This is why profanity makes such a useful decoy.

It is visible.

You can hear it immediately. Circle it. Censor it. Ban it. Flag it. Scold it. Put it in a policy manual and congratulate yourself for defending civilization from syllables with muddy boots.

Meanwhile, the failures that actually ruin lives often move quietly in the background.

Dishonesty.

Betrayal.

Cruelty.

Cowardice.

Greed.

Exploitation.

Manipulation.

Abuse of power.

Those things rarely announce themselves with a forbidden word and a cymbal crash. They wear suits. They use approved language. They issue statements. They say “deeply committed” and “shared values” and “moving forward” while character is being quietly dismembered behind the curtains.

This is how a society loses the plot.

It becomes more alert to surface violations than to substantive failures. It treats symbolic offenses as moral emergencies while deeper corruptions proceed with excellent punctuation. It teaches people which words might get them in trouble but not how to become trustworthy when no one is watching.

That is backwards.

A civilization should care less about whether a person can perform virtue and more about whether they possess it.

Not because words do not matter.

They do.

Not because courtesy is meaningless.

It is not.

Not because people should be free to be cruel and then hide behind “honesty” like a drunk raccoon behind a shower curtain.

They should not.

But morality and etiquette are not the same thing.

One teaches us how to behave in polite company.

The other determines who we become when polite company leaves the room.

That is the distinction we keep losing.

A person who never swears may still lie, exploit, flatter, manipulate, betray, and abandon. A person with a colorful vocabulary may still be honest, loyal, brave, generous, and present when everything goes sideways. Adults know this. Experience teaches it quickly, often while standing in a hospital hallway, beside a broken machine, after a storm, during a crisis, or in the quiet aftermath of discovering who actually came when called.

When life becomes real, performance loses its shine.

The friend who sits with you in the emergency room matters more than the acquaintance who always says the right thing.

The coworker who tells you an uncomfortable truth matters more than the supervisor who delivers polished nonsense.

The neighbor who shows up with tools, food, fuel, or a spare generator during a crisis matters more than the person who posts the correct sentiment online and then vanishes like a virtue-scented fart in a windstorm.

This does not mean we should become less civil.

It means we should become more honest about what civility can and cannot accomplish.

Courtesy can make shared life gentler. It can reduce unnecessary friction. It can signal respect. It can help people live together without turning every dinner table, workplace, comment thread, and school-board meeting into a cage fight with refreshments.

Good.

Keep courtesy.

But do not worship it.

Civility without character is just upholstery on a coffin.

Which brings me back to Jesus.

Or, more precisely, to the absurd image of a young carpenter smashing his thumb with a hammer while Joseph raises an eyebrow from across the shop.

Did it happen?

Probably not.

Maybe.

Who knows?

The historical evidence is disappointingly thin, which is rude of history but on brand.

What matters is the question the image raises.

If given a choice between a man who never swore and a bastard who would sacrifice his comfort, his time, his money, his reputation, and perhaps even his life to help another person, which one are we actually trying to produce?

Because that answer reveals far more about our moral imagination than any debate about profanity ever could.

The goal is not to make people vulgar.

The goal is to make people good.

And goodness, unfortunately for everyone who enjoys a simple checklist, cannot be reduced to vocabulary.

It has to be lived.

It has to be chosen.

It has to show up.

Preferably before the flood reaches the porch.

Preferably with tools.

And if the person carrying those tools mutters something unfit for synagogue use while saving your life, perhaps the proper response is not moral panic.

Perhaps the proper response is gratitude.
