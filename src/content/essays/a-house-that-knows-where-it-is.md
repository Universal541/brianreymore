In the life Kendra and I are working toward, there’s a cowboy camp at the top of our place in Arizona. I can see myself riding up from the barn with the dogs, lighting a fire, and putting something in the cauldron. Kendra’s there. The last light has gone out of the desert below us, and it’ll be a while before supper’s ready. For once, that’s fine. There’s nowhere else I need to be.

I think of it as calling a council, though I expect the other members will have the good sense to ignore the summons. They have their own business. Some may pass close enough for me to hear them. Most nights I might do all the talking by keeping my mouth shut.

I wanted to be Grizzly Adams when I was a kid. I think I wanted to walk through the woods and have some creature look up, see me, and go back to what it was doing. It’s a small thing to imagine. The older I get, the more I hear in it.

I’m longing for an encounter in which another feral living being recognizes my presence without fear, acknowledges me, and allows me to remain free, at least as much as I allow it to remain free. The word *allows* is strange there. I haven’t got the authority to grant a wild creature its freedom, and I suppose it hasn’t got any authority over mine. But it might know something about being free that I’ve forgotten. Perhaps, in the time it takes for us to look at each other, some of that comes back to me.

The fire is one evening. I’m after whatever I can carry down the hill in the morning. I want the peace Kendra and I find there to have some bearing on how we treat each other when there’s work to do and neither of us slept worth a damn. I want it in the shop and at the kitchen table. I want to know whether, given time, some of it might reach the parts of me that hurt.

For now, Kendra is posted up on the sofa, knees up, playing games on her iPhone. I’m in the recliner under a blanket, laptop open, talking with my AI assistant about this very essay you’re reading.

*Castle* is on the television. We’ve watched the whole series several times. It’s intelligent, well made, and familiar enough that we can pay attention or let it carry on without us. We already know what happens next.

It’s a comforting arrangement. Our own lives have been less forthcoming with the scripts.

We like to think we have some idea where we’re headed. We bring our best understanding, our best efforts and intentions, and sprinkle the whole questionable enterprise with hope that the world will go our way.

But we don’t know. Not really.

I’ve loved Looney Tunes and Bugs Bunny for as long as I can remember. There’s something reassuring about a rabbit who can emerge from a tunnel in entirely the wrong place, consult his map, and conclude that he should have taken that left turn at Albuquerque. He gives the predicament a moment’s consideration, then gets on with whatever lunacy is waiting aboveground.

I appreciate that more with every passing year.

Somewhere between the map and the destination, there’s the country you actually have to travel through. Kendra and I are in it now, making plans from a sofa and a recliner, with a familiar television show keeping us company.

Mr. Kitten is asleep on the bed. Later, when Mom and Dad finally join him, there will be the customary sassin’: hands and feet attacked through the blankets, the nightly defense of his kingdom against the outrageous presence of the people he’s been waiting for. Eventually, hostilities will cease. We’ll curl up together and drift into slumber land.

One small happy family.

One collective dream of desert nights spent at the cowboy camp we don’t own yet.

I wonder what Mr. Kitten would make of that place. I know what I hope it will mean for us. But there’s something here, in this ordinary evening, that I want to remember when I imagine Arizona: we already have a life worth carrying there.

The campfire matters to me. So does the woman on the sofa. So does the ridiculous little creature waiting to ambush us through the bedding.

Whatever freedom I find beneath those stars, I want us to feel at home in it together.

And that brings me back to the council I imagine at the top of the property. I would arrive with my human worries, my hopes, my considerable talent for getting tangled in things. Somewhere beyond the firelight, other lives would be carrying on according to understandings I barely possess.

I’d like to get quiet enough to learn something.

For now, we dream. And we work toward that dream.

We hold it loosely in our hands.

And ask that north find its way home to us.

Of course, I’m asking north to find me while sitting under a blanket with a computer in my lap.

The contradiction has not escaped me.

There’s a television across the room, a phone in Kendra’s hands, and an artificial intelligence helping me find words for my longing to reconnect with the living world. Somewhere, machines are consuming electricity so I can explain how much I want to sit beside a fire and listen.

It would be convenient if I hated technology. I could throw the laptop into a ravine, announce my spiritual advancement, and discover shortly afterward that I needed a map out of the ravine.

But I love tools. I’ve spent much of my life figuring out how things work, why they stopped working, and what some previous enthusiast did with the wiring. I know the satisfaction of making something useful with my hands. A machine doing its job well can be a beautiful thing.

There will be tools in Arizona. Computers. Music. Workshops. Things under construction that Kendra will reasonably inquire about finishing. I expect to arrive with a substantial collection of human ingenuity, including several items I probably should have thrown away three moves ago.

The question is what kind of life we arrange around them.

I want a house that knows where it is.

I want its windows to have something to do with the sun. I want to understand the water we use: where it comes from, how we keep it, where it goes. I want growing things close enough that their progress becomes part of my day. I want to learn the land well enough to notice when something changes.

Some of that will require technology. All of it will require attention.

And attention is harder to purchase.

I can buy equipment for measuring soil moisture and still fail to look at a plant. I can install a magnificent solar system and spend every hour of daylight indoors. I could build the entire dream and somehow remain unavailable for the life taking place inside it.

That possibility bothers me.

Arizona cannot do all the work. There will be mornings when I wake up there with the same crowded head I have here. A beautiful view will still be waiting outside while I convince myself I have seventeen things to finish before I’m entitled to see it.

I know the fellow who will be making that argument. He’s persuasive. He’s been running the place for years.

Perhaps that’s part of why the cowboy camp stays so vivid in my mind. I imagine going up there and allowing the day to continue without my supervision. The fire needs a little tending. The horse has its own opinions. Somewhere in the dark, a creature pauses to assess the noisy newcomer who apparently requires furniture and cooked supper to sit on a hill.

I hope I learn to be decent company.

When I picture that encounter with a wild animal, I feel the possibility of being received into a world that has carried on around me all my life. I want to recognize it more fully, and to be recognized within it. I want to know what happens to my sense of myself when I’m quiet enough to let another being set the terms of its approach.

It may keep its distance. That distance would be part of the conversation.

And if it stays, if we share a little time without either of us needing anything from the other, I imagine carrying that experience home to Kendra. There might be very little to tell.

Something came by.

We watched each other.

For a while, I felt at home.

I think that’s where some of the ache lives: in how deeply I want that feeling, and how strange it seems that a creature born of this Earth should have to work so hard to recover it.

We come from this place. Whatever else we believe about ourselves, we spend our lives taking it into our bodies. Water, food, breath. Eventually, what we’ve borrowed returns.

Ashes to ashes. Dust to dust.

There is an intimacy in that which I find difficult to reconcile with the way we sometimes speak of the Earth—as property, inventory, an obstacle, a supply cupboard with an unfortunate number of species in the way.

I use those cupboards too. I have bills to pay and materials to buy. My hands are thoroughly involved in the world I’m questioning.

But I want to pay closer attention to what passes through them.

I want the house, the workshops, the garden, and the life Kendra and I make there to help us practice that attention. I want the ordinary acts of keeping ourselves alive to bring us into a better acquaintance with what sustains us.

And I would like, while there is still time, to become more familiar with the place my dust is going.

And yet, when I picture myself finally sitting beside that fire, I know I’ll still be thinking about the people who haven’t found a place to rest.

I won’t become unaware of them because I’ve acquired a horse.

There will still be people working themselves into the ground to remain barely above it. People spending their best hours earning permission to occupy a small piece of the planet they were born on. People so tired by evening that asking them to reconnect with nature sounds like assigning homework to somebody who’s already asleep at the kitchen table.

I recognize something of myself in them.

It’s difficult to cultivate a spacious inner life when every available room is occupied by an overdue bill.

That’s part of what hurts when I look around. I see people longing for relief, then being offered another thing to buy, another way to optimize themselves, another explanation of how their exhaustion reflects a failure of discipline. Even rest arrives with instructions. We’ve managed to make breathing an activity you can fall behind on.

I would like us to have more room than that.

I want it for Kendra. I want days in which we can work hard at things we care about and still have something left when we find each other in the evening. I want to look at her without half my attention keeping watch for the next emergency. I want us to grow older with opportunities to be curious, foolish, affectionate, and occasionally useless.

Especially useless.

There ought to be afternoons when nobody has to justify the hammock.

I don’t expect the homestead to deliver an exemption from trouble. Things will break there. Weather will have opinions about my plans. I will undoubtedly spend some portion of my remaining life standing beside an impressive hole, explaining why it was necessary.

But I hope the work will put us in closer touch with its purpose. A repair that keeps our water flowing. Food growing because we tended it. A place made comfortable for someone we love. Enough of those connections in a day, perhaps, that the day feels like something we inhabited.

And I hope we’ll have room to welcome people.

There’s an interesting tension in this dream of mine. I want privacy, quiet, and distance from a great deal of human nonsense. I also keep imagining places for people to sit.

Apparently, even my retreat requires extra chairs.

I can see someone arriving worn down, carrying whatever the world has been handing them. We might talk. We might work on something in the shop. Later, there could be food and a fire, and a stretch of evening in which nobody needs them to explain why they’re having a hard time.

I know what it can mean to be given that much room.

The same wish reaches beyond people, into that council I imagine in the dark. I want to become more attentive to the lives already there: where they travel, what they need, when my presence becomes an interruption. If I’m going to call a place home, I ought to learn something about the neighbors who won’t be coming over to borrow a socket wrench.

That feels like a reasonable beginning.

There’s so much I don’t know. Some of my longing reaches into spiritual territory I’m still exploring. I feel connections I struggle to describe. I want to develop the quiet, the practice, and the patience to understand more of what I’m experiencing.

I can live with having questions.

What wears on me is the expectation that we should hurry past them and get back to something productive.

I would like to stay with a few. What makes a place feel safe enough for a living being to rest? What happens when we give each other enough room? How much of a life can change when attention becomes a daily practice?

The cowboy camp is where I imagine sitting with those questions. Its firelight reaches only a little way. Beyond it, there would be a whole landscape I’d still be learning.

I like to think of Kendra beside me, the dogs settling, the horse shifting its weight. Somewhere down the hill, our house would be holding the day’s warmth.

For a little while, I could let it.

I could sit there with everyone I love who happened to be present, and everyone I love who came to mind, and give the evening my undivided attention.

Eventually, somebody would have to get up and put another piece of wood on the fire.

I find that detail reassuring.

Even in the life I dream about, the fire needs tending. The profound moment has a practical requirement. You can spend a very moving half hour contemplating your relationship with the universe and still discover that you’ve been sitting on a rock.

I suspect I’ll be good at that part.

Reverence has to make room for a man adjusting his ass.

Perhaps that’s why I keep coming back to a place I can build and work in. I need somewhere for these feelings to go through my hands. A thought about belonging can become a decision about where to put a path. Concern for water can become an afternoon spent figuring out how to catch it. Love can become a meal, a repaired latch, a chair placed where Kendra likes the view.

I understand those acts. They give me something to do with feelings that can otherwise become too large to carry.

Because sometimes I look at what we do to one another, and to the places that sustain us, and I don’t know where to put the hurt. It follows me into an ordinary evening. I can be safe for the moment, beside someone I love, and still feel the weight of suffering well beyond our room.

I don’t claim to understand all of that feeling. I know that it’s there.

The dream gives it somewhere to work. There is land to care for, a life to make gentler, something worth learning. I can imagine waking up and putting my effort into those things. I can imagine discovering, over years, how much I’ve misunderstood.

I hope I’m still willing to be surprised.

There’s a great deal of confidence in the human world. Everybody seems to have a map. Some are selling theirs. A few appear to have drawn the destination around wherever they were already standing.

Bugs Bunny, at least, was willing to look around and acknowledge a navigational problem.

I’ve loved that rabbit most of my life. He could be magnificently sure of himself and still emerge from the ground somewhere he hadn’t intended to go. There was always the moment of looking: at the map, at the surroundings, at the rather serious discrepancy between them.

I think we could afford to spend a little longer in that moment.

I’m trying to spend some time there myself. What do I want to carry into the rest of my life? What have I been carrying simply because I’ve had it so long? Which parts of this dream can I begin practicing while the land is still beyond our reach?

Here in the recliner, the answer is smaller than a homestead.

I can lower the laptop.

Kendra is still on the sofa. *Castle* carries on with the comforting competence of people whose difficulties will be resolved before the credits. Mr. Kitten has no interest in the essay and every expectation that we will eventually report for blanket combat.

There is a whole evening here asking to be lived.

I want those Arizona nights with an ache that sometimes catches me by surprise. I want the ride up the hill, the fire, the long acquaintance with a piece of Earth. I want to find out who I become with enough time to listen.

And when we finally make that ride, I want to recognize the happiness beside me. We’ve been keeping some of it alive here, through the worry and the work, on a sofa and in a recliner, in the nightly company of a cat.

We have somewhere to begin.

For tonight, I’ll put this down and go join my family.

There are feet to be attacked.

North knows where to find us.
