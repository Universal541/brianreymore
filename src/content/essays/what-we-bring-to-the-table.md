*By Brian Reymore*

Somewhere in America, a person is standing in a kitchen insisting that everything is under control while holding a potato masher like a weapon of last resort.

There are too many people near the oven. Someone has offered to help and been assigned the highly technical responsibility of going somewhere else. A recipe has reached the stage at which measurements give way to ancestral authority. Somewhere, a smoke alarm is contributing its opinion.

And people are coming.

People we love. People we worry about. People with whom we share a resemblance, a complicated history, and an agreement to leave certain subjects alone until at least the pie.

For some of us, this is Thanksgiving.

For others, the day is quieter. A shift at work. A meal stretched carefully. An empty chair that still changes the shape of the room. A holiday whose familiar story asks us to celebrate something our own family history teaches us to mourn.

We arrive at the same date carrying very different things.

If Thanksgiving is going to mean something worthy of the name, we ought to make room for what people actually bring.

Many of us were handed a small, tidy story: Pilgrims, Indians, a difficult winter, a helpful neighbor, a harvest feast. The paper hats were optional. The reassuring conclusion was generally compulsory.

There was a gathering in 1621. Edward Winslow’s brief account describes three days of feasting with Ousamequin, often called Massasoit, and about ninety of his men, who contributed five deer. That surviving English eyewitness account leaves much unanswered. It cannot tell us everything the Wampanoag participants understood, intended, or felt. A colonial record is a source to examine, not a window through which every person present becomes equally visible.[^1]

The people the newcomers encountered already had names, communities, political relationships, and long traditions of giving thanks. The Wampanoag were making consequential decisions about an alliance in their own homeland. Gratitude had been practiced there long before anyone arrived to give it an English name.[^2]

And the landscape had already suffered terribly. Epidemics brought through European contact devastated Native communities before the Mayflower arrived. The colonists established Plymouth at Patuxet, a Wampanoag village emptied by that catastrophe.[^3]

Pause there for a moment.

Before the familiar picture of abundance, there were households that would never gather again.

There were people who had known the sound of particular voices and would not hear them anymore. Children, parents, elders. Ordinary lives with all the private significance our own lives possess.

They deserve more from us than a sentence hurried past on the way to the centerpiece.

The decades following the gathering brought colonial expansion, dispossession, and devastating warfare. In December 1675, English colonial forces attacked a Narragansett winter settlement sheltering Wampanoag refugees. The Great Swamp Massacre killed hundreds, including women and children.[^4] The wider conflict known as King Philip’s War brought further death, displacement, and enslavement.[^3]

The feast, the later wars, and the much later national holiday are distinct events. Collapsing them into one scene would give us another misleading story. But cutting the meal loose from everything that followed leaves us with a comforting picture purchased through omission.

We can grieve what happened without making a performance of how deeply we grieve it. We can begin by learning whose lives were involved, listening to their descendants, and resisting the temptation to tidy the room before everyone has spoken.

The descendants are here.

Wampanoag people have communities, governments, families, cultural gatherings, and language reclamation work today.[^3] They are people with a present and a future, including the right to tell their own history and disagree with the version we prefer.

In 1970, Wamsutta Frank James, an Aquinnah Wampanoag man, was invited to speak at a Massachusetts commemoration of the Pilgrims’ arrival. When organizers objected to his account of colonial harms and sought different remarks, he refused. The gathering that followed in Plymouth became the first National Day of Mourning. United American Indians of New England has organized the observance annually since then.[^5]

There is a painful lesson in inviting someone to a celebration of brotherhood and then discovering that his memories are inconvenient to the program.

We ought to remember it when we say everyone is welcome.

No single response to Thanksgiving represents every Indigenous person. We should leave people the freedom to celebrate, mourn, gather with family, protest, or experience several of those things together. A national holiday can survive being understood differently by the people who live through it.

Our affection ought to be sturdy enough to listen.

The November holiday itself took shape much later than the gathering at Plymouth. In 1863, after Sarah Josepha Hale’s long campaign for a national observance, Abraham Lincoln called for a day of Thanksgiving amid the Civil War.[^6]

His proclamation expressed gratitude, but it also acknowledged national wrongdoing and asked Americans to remember widows, orphans, mourners, and sufferers. It called for the wounds of the nation to be healed.[^6]

That is a considerably more demanding exercise than announcing that everything is wonderful and passing the rolls.

The country was tearing itself apart. Gratitude was being asked to exist within grief, responsibility, and uncertainty about what came next.

There is something in that worth keeping.

We don’t need to borrow Lincoln’s religious language to understand the human possibility. At a table where people pray differently, or do not pray, there can still be a shared recognition: we have been sustained, we have caused harm, we have work before us, and we are fortunate to have another day in which to attempt it.

I find that moving.

I find America moving in much the same difficult way.

There is something astonishing about this unfinished experiment in people governing themselves: the possibility that authority can be questioned, that a citizen can argue with the country’s account of itself, that an inherited arrangement can be challenged by the people required to live under it.

That possibility has been denied to far too many, and exercising it has demanded sacrifices that comfortable people sometimes mistake for an inevitable march of progress.

Nothing about the better chapters was inevitable.

People had to insist. Organize. Endure. Return after defeat. Tell the truth while being informed that their tone was unfortunate.

Much of what I love in America lives in that persistence: people taking its promises seriously enough to demand that they become available to someone else.

I am grateful for the chance to take part in that work. For the ordinary freedom to write this, to question what I was taught, to learn something that unsettles me, and to hope that putting it into words might be useful.

That is an extraordinary inheritance, even with the unfinished obligations attached.

We are here. The country has made it this far. For all the cruelty, foolishness, courage, generosity, and contradiction that brought us to this moment, there are still people arriving who have not yet decided what America will mean in their lives.

We get to help shape their answer.

That fills me with gratitude. It also makes me want to be careful with what I hand them.

A child should be able to love a place and learn its history without being taught that one must destroy the other. We can tell children about generosity and betrayal, about people helping each other survive and people choosing to take what belonged to someone else. We can acknowledge what we still do not know.

They will eventually discover that adults left things out. I would rather they remember us as people willing to keep learning with them.

And they will learn from more than the history we tell.

They will notice who cooks, who sits, and who gets thanked. Whether the person washing dishes ever gets to finish a story. Whether the relative with less money is treated as an equal or as a seasonal improvement project. Whether someone can say they are having a hard year without being instructed to appreciate it more enthusiastically.

Children are remarkably observant when adults are busy congratulating themselves on their values.

The turkey is unlikely to distract them indefinitely.

For a comfortable household, Thanksgiving may offer abundance so familiar that it becomes scenery. For someone counting the cost of every grocery bag, the same meal can represent weeks of calculation. For a person alone, the invitation may matter more than anything served. For someone mourning, simply getting through the afternoon can be enough.

And somewhere, someone is working so that another person can rest. A caregiver. A cook. A person cleaning up after a celebration they had no opportunity to share.

I would like our gratitude to reach them while they are still within hearing distance.

Being thankful for having enough becomes a more serious practice when we ask why someone else does not. A holiday meal can relieve hunger for an evening. Our obligations to one another continue on Friday.

So does our responsibility to the people whose histories we have finally agreed to hear. A more accurate story belongs alongside respect for living Native nations, their rights, their land, and their authority over their own affairs. Listening should have consequences in how we behave.

We inherit a holiday through repetition. We give it meaning through practice.

Some of that practice can be very small. Make an invitation without demanding cheerfulness in return. Send food home without turning generosity into a transaction. Learn the name of the people whose history you were taught to summarize as scenery. Let an uncomfortable fact stay in the conversation long enough to change your understanding.

Some of it requires work far beyond the table: making our communities more livable, our institutions more answerable, and our promises less dependent on who can afford to enforce them.

The meal cannot carry all of that. It can remind us why it matters.

I want Thanksgiving to have room for joy. The unguarded kind. Somebody laughing hard enough to lose the thread of the story. The pleasure of feeding a person who takes one bite and closes their eyes. A familiar hand finding yours beneath the table.

There is no betrayal in loving those moments.

There is something worth protecting in our capacity to make them for one another, especially in a world that has given people ample reason to be afraid.

We can honor the dead and feed the living. We can feel sorrow for what our predecessors did and awe at what human beings have managed to repair. We can be thankful that this country has endured while recognizing that endurance alone will never tell us whether we have used our time well.

The people coming after us will have their own Thanksgiving tables. They will bring recipes, family stories, empty chairs, and questions we have not thought to ask. They will live with some of our decisions long after they have forgotten our names.

I hope we leave them more to be grateful for.

More honest history. More dependable freedom. More room to belong. A little less inherited cruelty dressed up as common sense. Enough courage to change a tradition when love asks something better of it.

I hope they find evidence that we understood how fortunate we were to have a turn at this extraordinary, troubled, still-possible country.

For now, there is food to tend and someone we ought to call. There is a story worth hearing from the person who has been quiet. There is grief that needs company and happiness that deserves to be enjoyed while it is here.

We can bring all of that to the table.

I want to be grateful in a way that somebody else can feel.

And when those coming behind us ask what Thanksgiving meant to our generation, I hope there will be people who can answer:

They made room for us.

[^1]: Plimoth Patuxet Museums, [“Eye-Witness Account of the 1621 Harvest Celebration”](https://plimoth.org/yath/unit-4/eye-witness-account-of-the-1621-harvest-celebration), including Edward Winslow’s letter published in *Mourt’s Relation* (1622). The surviving account leaves participants’ motives and many details uncertain.

[^2]: Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian, [“Rethinking Thanksgiving Celebrations: Native Perspectives on Thanksgiving”](https://americanindian.si.edu/nk360/informational/rethinking-thanksgiving). [^3]: Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian, [“The ‘First Thanksgiving’: Timeline”](https://americanindian.si.edu/nk360/Thanksgiving/timeline.html), a Wampanoag-informed educational resource. Its account of living communities is not an exhaustive current inventory of tribal recognition or status.

[^4]: National Park Service, Roger Williams National Memorial, [“Roger Williams: King Philip’s War”](https://www.nps.gov/rowi/learn/historyculture/philipswar.htm). Describes the attack on the Narragansett winter settlement and its Wampanoag refugees in December 1675.

[^5]: Mashpee Wampanoag Tribe, [“National Day of Mourning”](https://mashpeewampanoagtribe-nsn.gov/2022-november-mittark-blog/2022/10/26/53rd-national-day-of-mourning); United American Indians of New England, [“Historical Information”](https://www.uaine.org/historical.htm). Tribal and organizer accounts of Wamsutta Frank James’s rejected speech and the annual observance beginning in 1970.

[^6]: National Archives, [“President Abraham Lincoln’s Thanksgiving Day Proclamation”](https://visit.archives.gov/whats-on/explore-exhibits/president-abraham-lincolns-thanksgiving-day-proclamation), October 3, 1863. The proclamation and accompanying historical introduction document the national observance, Sarah Josepha Hale’s advocacy, penitence, mourning, and the appeal for healing.
