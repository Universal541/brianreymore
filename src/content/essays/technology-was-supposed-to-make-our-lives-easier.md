*The unpaid employee economy, the industrialization of helplessness, and the peculiar disappearance of human beings from systems designed to serve them.*

## An Expedition of Approximately Two Thousand Dollars

On the twenty-fifth of November, 2026, the day before Thanksgiving, I embarked upon an expedition that should have required neither provisions nor advanced navigational equipment. I went to a credit union to open a business checking account.

There was something rather appropriate about the occasion, given that the business in question was Expedition Growth Lab LLC. I had anticipated celebrating another small milestone in the development of a company dedicated, among other things, to helping businesses develop more intelligent, efficient, resilient operating systems. What I received instead was a remarkably well-orchestrated demonstration of why such a company needs to exist.

The adventure required an unexpected return home to retrieve an updated operating agreement, while Kendra had to undertake a separate documentary exercise to establish where I lived. There were disclosures, signatures, questions, verifications, and the seemingly inexhaustible supply of little boxes upon which modern civilization has apparently decided to establish its foundations. I remain reasonably confident that, had Christopher Columbus been required to complete the contemporary equivalent of these procedures before departing Spain, the Americas would have enjoyed several additional centuries of relative peace.

Eventually, we reached the comparatively uncomplicated business of putting money into the account.

I had a check for two thousand dollars from TrueNorth Facility Services LLC, representing payment of an invoice issued by Expedition Growth Lab for startup and business development consulting services. Two legitimate businesses, one legitimate commercial transaction, one legitimate financial instrument, and the two human beings principally responsible for the arrangement physically present and sufficiently conscious to explain it. We had, in other words, assembled virtually everything humanity ought to require to move a modest sum of money from one place to another.

The credit union informed us that the check would be subject to a nine-business-day hold, with the funds expected to become available on December ninth. However, there was a possibility that the hold could be lifted if we could demonstrate that sufficient funds were available in TrueNorth's account. This was encouraging. Somebody had apparently incorporated an exception into the machinery, thereby acknowledging the possibility that circumstances might occasionally require something resembling human judgment.

Kendra opened her banking application. Her business account wasn't there.

She logged into the institution's website. Her business account wasn't there either.

She subsequently attempted to withdraw cash through an ATM, whereupon the machine informed her that her PIN was invalid.

The account existed. We understood the money to be there. Its owner was present. The receiving credit union was apparently willing to reconsider the hold. Yet the infrastructure designed to facilitate the verification necessary to accomplish that reconsideration had become the principal obstacle to accomplishing it.

Somewhere, presumably, a computer knew everything we needed to know. Unfortunately, none of the human beings involved could persuade the relevant machinery to admit it.

Decades ago, under certain circumstances, I could telephone a financial institution and obtain verification concerning a check. That verification was not a guarantee that the check would ultimately clear, and financial institutions understandably maintain restrictions upon the information they disclose. Nevertheless, there were circumstances in which one human being could communicate sufficient information to another human being to facilitate a relatively ordinary commercial transaction.

Today, humanity possesses communications technology capable of transmitting extraordinary quantities of information around the planet almost instantaneously. We have placed functioning laboratories on Mars, constructed machines capable of examining the molecular architecture of living organisms, developed artificial intelligence capable of conversing about practically every subject of human inquiry, and established global financial networks through which enormous sums of money move electronically.

And there we were, two days before the first Thanksgiving leftovers would require refrigeration, unable to establish through the available machinery that money could be moved from one account to another.

What the fuck happened?

## The Miracle That Ate Its Own Instructions

There is a tendency, when discussing technology, to confuse the sophistication of an invention with the intelligence of its implementation. A machine capable of accomplishing something extraordinary is presumed to confer some measure of extraordinary intelligence upon the people employing it. This assumption is particularly entertaining when those people manage to organize its employment in such a way that it becomes more difficult to accomplish something that was relatively straightforward before the machine arrived.

Consider the humble check. In 2003, Congress passed the Check Clearing for the 21st Century Act, commonly known as Check 21, which became effective in October 2004. Its purpose was to reduce legal obstacles to electronic check processing, eliminating much of the necessity of physically transporting pieces of paper between financial institutions. According to the Federal Reserve, the number of its paper check-processing offices declined from forty-five in 2003 to one in 2010 as electronic processing became widespread. Interbank check collection subsequently became almost entirely electronic.[1]

This was an entirely sensible technological development. Moving an electronic representation of a financial instrument instead of transporting the physical instrument itself saves time, labor, transportation costs, and administrative effort. Nobody should be particularly nostalgic about the expense of flying a collection of checks across the country in an airplane when the information those checks contain can travel electronically.

Yet technological capability and the availability of deposited funds are two different things.

Federal regulations governing funds availability, principally Regulation CC, establish requirements and exceptions concerning different types of deposits and accounts. Qualifying new accounts are subject to special provisions during their first thirty days. Ordinary commercial checks deposited into such accounts can be held longer than they otherwise might be; indeed, the Federal Reserve's official commentary explains that the ordinary availability schedules do not establish a maximum hold period for certain checks under the new-account exception. The familiar ninth-business-day provision applies specifically to portions of certain otherwise favored check deposits.[2]

Consequently, the nine-business-day hold we encountered should not be confused with a universal federal requirement that every newly deposited two-thousand-dollar business check must spend nine business days contemplating its existence.

Financial institutions have legitimate reasons to manage the risks associated with new accounts, counterfeit checks, insufficient funds, fraudulent activity, and transactions whose authenticity has not yet been established. A snapshot of an account balance cannot guarantee that funds will remain available when a check is presented for payment. I understand the underlying problem. What interests me is the extraordinary disconnect between the technical capabilities we have developed and the administrative architecture through which those capabilities are made available to ordinary people.

We had developed a sufficiently sophisticated system to identify a potential exception, but the surrounding infrastructure was unable to support the exception's practical execution.

That is a design failure, regardless of whether every individual component was operating according to its instructions.

The people working at the credit union were working within the system available to them. They did not design the software, establish the national regulatory framework, negotiate the relevant banking technology contracts, or determine the manner in which another institution's business accounts would appear on its customers' mobile devices. Indeed, their willingness to consider an exception was one of the more encouraging aspects of the entire experience.

The absurdity was architectural.

And once you begin looking at modern civilization through that particular lens, the credit union starts looking less like an isolated inconvenience and more like an unusually concise illustration of the world we've been building.

## John Maynard Keynes and the Missing Fucking Afternoon

In 1930, during the early years of the Great Depression, economist John Maynard Keynes published an essay entitled *Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren*. Keynes was contemplating the long-term implications of technological advancement, productivity, capital accumulation, and humanity's capacity to satisfy its material needs.

Among the possibilities he envisioned was a fifteen-hour working week. He suggested that, within approximately a century, technological and economic progress might permit human beings in prosperous societies to devote considerably less of their lives to satisfying the economic necessities of existence and considerably more to the business of actually existing.[3]

Keynes did not anticipate that his grandchildren would spend their liberated afternoons attempting to establish whether a password contained a sufficiently diverse collection of punctuation marks.

To be fair to technological progress, many of its promises have been fulfilled. Agricultural machinery has relieved enormous quantities of physical labor. Medical technologies have extended lives and improved their quality. Domestic appliances have reduced the effort required to accomplish numerous household tasks. Communication systems have made it possible for families separated by oceans to speak face-to-face. Working hours in many industrialized societies have declined over the long historical arc, owing to a combination of technological progress, labor organization, legislation, economic development, and changing social expectations.[4]

The technological revolution has produced real human benefits. I have absolutely no desire to surrender refrigeration, modern dentistry, or the ability to communicate with somebody several thousand miles away without first procuring a particularly ambitious pigeon.

Nevertheless, Keynes's grandchildren inherited something he might have found rather perplexing: an enormous secondary economy of administrative labor that accompanied the very technologies intended to reduce their workload.

Consider the contemporary household. Its occupants maintain relationships with numerous financial institutions, insurance companies, healthcare providers, telecommunications businesses, utilities, government agencies, employers, retailers, subscription services, and assorted technology companies. Each relationship can come with its own application, website, authentication requirements, notification preferences, billing procedures, security policies, software updates, and methods for resolving problems.

The household has effectively become a small administrative office.

Its employees are unpaid, frequently untrained, and expected to maintain functioning relationships with numerous institutions whose internal operating procedures they have neither the authority to influence nor the opportunity to examine. They are responsible for tracking changes to those procedures, acquiring compatible equipment, protecting credentials, updating information, identifying errors, pursuing corrections, and ensuring that their failure to notice something buried in an email does not result in the termination of an essential service.

At the conclusion of a normal working day, an individual may return home to commence another shift as the accounts receivable department, information technology specialist, insurance claims administrator, compliance officer, procurement manager, and customer-service representative for their own existence.

There is something deeply peculiar about a civilization in which the average citizen requires an increasingly sophisticated understanding of administrative systems simply to be left alone long enough to enjoy dinner.

Where did Keynes's afternoon go?

I suspect a considerable portion of it is sitting on hold.

## Welcome to the Company. You Will Not Be Paid.

In 1981, philosopher and social critic Ivan Illich published *Shadow Work*, examining forms of unpaid labor that accompany and support the formal economic system. More recently, researchers have applied related concepts to the expanding world of self-service technology and the labor customers perform in the course of purchasing and using products and services.[5]

The distinction becomes particularly interesting when we examine the transformation of the retail checkout.

For most of its history, the conventional supermarket employed people to operate its cash registers. The customer brought merchandise to the register, the employee processed the transaction, and the customer paid. It was a comparatively comprehensible division of responsibilities.

Then came the self-checkout machine, an extraordinary technological advancement through which a retailer could persuade its customers to perform a portion of its employees' work without having to put them on the payroll.

Self-checkout can be genuinely convenient. I have used it voluntarily and appreciated the opportunity to complete a small purchase without waiting behind somebody conducting the annual grocery procurement exercise for a moderately sized republic. There are circumstances in which it serves the customer and the retailer simultaneously.

Then there are circumstances in which the retailer closes most of its staffed registers, directs customers toward self-service, and effectively eliminates the distinction between an optional convenience and a mandatory contribution of unpaid labor.

This is when the arrangement becomes particularly fascinating.

The customer locates the merchandise, transports it to the register, scans it, identifies the varieties of produce involved, bags the purchase, processes the payment, and resolves whatever minor neurological episode the machine experiences upon encountering an onion.

If the onion subsequently occupies an unauthorized location within the bagging area, the customer must summon an employee whose principal responsibility appears to have evolved from conducting retail transactions to negotiating peace treaties between vegetables and software.

And should something go wrong, the customer may also be expected to demonstrate that the discrepancy did not originate with them.

Congratulations. You've joined the company.

There are no wages, benefits, paid holidays, or employee discounts associated with your appointment, and should you demonstrate sufficient enthusiasm for the position by completing the transaction successfully, the company may record your efforts as an improvement in its operational efficiency.

Researchers examining self-service technologies have identified precisely this transfer of work between organizations and customers, including the resentment that can arise when consumers perceive the arrangement as an imposition rather than a benefit.[6]

The distinction between useful self-service and imposed shadow work is relatively straightforward. If the arrangement saves me time, gives me control, and offers an accessible alternative when I need assistance, I have participated in a mutually beneficial technological development. If it primarily reduces the company's expenses while transferring its responsibilities to me, I have participated in a labor arrangement whose commercial terms somebody neglected to mention.

The underlying technology may be identical.

The underlying relationship is not.

## A Remarkable Disappearance From the Balance Sheet

Economists have long examined the costs associated with conducting transactions. Finding information, evaluating alternatives, negotiating agreements, completing paperwork, coordinating activities, and resolving disputes all consume resources. Those resources remain economically significant whether or not they appear in a conventional financial statement.

In 2015, Donald Moynihan, Pamela Herd, and Hope Harvey published an influential examination of administrative burden, identifying three broad categories of costs imposed upon people interacting with government: the effort required to learn about requirements and opportunities, the effort required to comply with them, and the psychological consequences of navigating the resulting arrangements.[7]

These categories are wonderfully applicable to the contemporary commercial world.

A person attempting to resolve an erroneous insurance charge must first determine what has happened, identify the responsible institution, discover the appropriate procedure, and establish what documentation is necessary. They must then assemble that documentation, communicate with the institution, pursue the matter through whichever departmental obstacles present themselves, and perhaps repeat portions of the process when information fails to arrive at its intended destination.

Throughout the experience, they may encounter frustration, anxiety, embarrassment, financial uncertainty, and the exceptionally unpleasant sensation of possessing a legitimate grievance without possessing a practical means of having it addressed.

The institution may have incurred a relatively modest administrative expense. The individual may have lost a substantial portion of their week.

This raises an interesting question concerning how we calculate efficiency.

Suppose a company previously employed people to resolve customer problems through direct communication. It introduces automated systems, reduces staffing, and records significant savings. Its shareholders are pleased. Its executives receive recognition for modernizing operations. The software vendor publishes a case study describing the impressive results of the implementation.

Meanwhile, customers collectively spend thousands of additional hours navigating the new machinery, correcting its mistakes, supplying information the institution already possesses, and attempting to reach the diminishing number of human beings available to intervene.

Where, exactly, are those thousands of hours recorded?

The company's accounting accurately reflects its own reduced expenditures. It may tell us absolutely nothing about whether the transaction has become more efficient when examined in its entirety.

The company hasn't necessarily eliminated the work. It may simply have relocated the work to people whose time it does not have to purchase.

This is the administrative equivalent of solving a waste-disposal problem by tipping the contents of your rubbish bin over the neighbor's fence. Your property looks considerably cleaner. Your waste-management expenses have declined. If you limit the geographical boundaries of your analysis to your own garden, the results are fucking magnificent.

The neighbor may arrive at a somewhat different assessment.

Yet modern corporate efficiency is frequently discussed within precisely those geographical boundaries. We count the institution's expenditure of time, money, and labor while treating the customer's corresponding expenditure as an unfortunate but economically invisible feature of the arrangement.

It is difficult to imagine a more convenient accounting system for the people designing the inconvenience.

## Sludge

Behavioral economists and legal scholars have introduced a particularly satisfying term for some of the unnecessary friction embedded within administrative and commercial processes: sludge.

Where a well-designed process helps people accomplish an objective with reasonable effort, sludge imposes obstacles that make the objective more difficult to achieve. Those obstacles can involve complicated instructions, unnecessary forms, inconvenient procedures, excessive delays, confusing choices, or a combination of requirements whose individual absurdities become apparent only when somebody attempts to complete the entire sequence.[8]

Sometimes the sludge is accidental. Sometimes it represents the accumulated residue of years of poorly coordinated decisions. And sometimes it has been deliberately positioned where a company stands to benefit from the customer's inability to move through it.

Consider the remarkable technological disparity between subscribing to a service and canceling it.

The modern commercial enterprise can often establish a recurring financial relationship with a customer in less time than it takes to sneeze. It can recognize the customer, obtain payment details, initiate billing, establish an account, send confirmation, and commence extracting money from the customer's financial infrastructure without requiring the intervention of a single employee.

The moment the customer wishes to terminate that relationship, however, the organization occasionally develops the administrative capabilities of a nineteenth-century village whose only telegraph operator has been kicked in the head by a horse.

Suddenly, communication must occur during particular business hours. A representative must be consulted. The customer must answer questions about their reasons for leaving, consider additional offers, navigate another webpage, confirm their identity, or undertake some other adventure whose necessity was conspicuously absent when the institution was acquiring permission to take their money.

In 2022, the Federal Trade Commission published a report examining manipulative digital design practices commonly referred to as dark patterns. Among the documented practices were methods for making subscriptions difficult to cancel, obscuring charges, manipulating consent, and encouraging consumers to disclose information they might otherwise prefer to retain.[9]

This is technology functioning exactly as intended. The problem is the intention.

In October 2024, the FTC adopted amendments to its Negative Option Rule intended, among other things, to make cancellation of recurring subscriptions comparably straightforward to enrollment. Business and industry groups challenged the rule, and on July 8, 2025, the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit vacated it on procedural grounds. In March 2026, the FTC initiated another rulemaking process concerning these practices.[10]

The procedural obligations of a government agency are legitimate subjects for judicial examination. What interests me is that enough commercial interests were sufficiently opposed to the regulatory initiative that the matter became a substantial legal undertaking.

Consider the technological civilization we inhabit. We possess the capability to initiate and terminate a recurring financial transaction with extraordinary simplicity. Nevertheless, establishing a broadly applicable expectation that companies allow their customers to leave without unnecessary obstacles has required investigations, regulatory proceedings, litigation, judicial review, and another round of regulatory proceedings.

We have apparently become so advanced that clicking a fucking button requires a constitutional civilization to assemble its legal resources.

Meanwhile, the subscriptions continue.

## The Industrialization of Helplessness

In 1983, human-factors researcher Lisanne Bainbridge published a paper entitled *Ironies of Automation*. Her central observation was that automation can introduce new problems for the human operators expected to supervise automated systems, particularly when the machinery encounters circumstances it cannot handle independently.[11]

As systems take over routine operations, the human operator may have fewer opportunities to maintain the practical experience necessary to intervene effectively. Yet that same individual may be expected to assume responsibility precisely when the situation has become unusually complicated.

This was an important observation in industrial process control. It has become an almost painfully appropriate description of contemporary customer service.

An organization automates a series of routine procedures. Its employees become responsible for assisting customers whose circumstances fit those procedures. As time progresses, the employees' practical responsibilities become increasingly defined by the software, their knowledge increasingly confined to the functions they are trained to perform, and their authority increasingly restricted by the permissions associated with their user accounts.

Eventually, something happens that the software's designers failed to anticipate.

A customer arrives with circumstances that do not fit the available options. An account exists but does not appear where it is supposed to appear. A payment has been received but has not been attributed correctly. An address has changed in one system but not another. A person has supplied documentation that establishes the necessary facts but does not possess the particular document the software has been instructed to accept.

The employee recognizes the problem. The customer recognizes the problem. Occasionally, several employees and a supervisor recognize the problem simultaneously.

Unfortunately, none of them possesses sufficient authority to resolve it.

There is a particularly humiliating quality to watching an intelligent, otherwise competent human being explain that they understand perfectly well what needs to happen but cannot accomplish it because an electronic system will not permit them to do so.

The employee has become a biological peripheral attached to institutional software.

We should examine what that arrangement says about the institution's relationship with its own workforce. We supposedly hire people because they possess knowledge, judgment, interpersonal abilities, and the capacity to respond intelligently to circumstances. We subsequently place them inside systems that may prevent them from employing those abilities whenever doing so would require deviation from a predetermined procedure.

Then we wonder why customer-service employees become exhausted, disengaged, and demoralized.

It is difficult to imagine a more effective method of destroying a person's professional confidence than repeatedly placing them in situations where they can see the correct solution but are prohibited from implementing it.

The institution has hired a human being, retained many of the expenses associated with employing a human being, and deliberately restricted the characteristics that made employing a human being worthwhile.

We have constructed an extraordinarily expensive machine for manufacturing helplessness.

## The Computer Says No

There is a distinction between automation and intelligence that seems increasingly necessary to establish.

Automation performs a process according to its design. Intelligence, whether human or artificial, involves capacities for interpreting circumstances, recognizing relationships, adapting to information, and producing responses that may extend beyond a predetermined sequence.

A conventional automated process can be wonderfully efficient at evaluating whether a collection of conditions has been satisfied. It becomes considerably less useful when the appropriate response requires questioning the conditions themselves.

Suppose the purpose of a verification process is to establish that somebody owns a particular financial account. The system requires a code sent to the person's mobile telephone. Unfortunately, the person's telephone has been lost, damaged, stolen, or disconnected. The individual arrives at a branch with identification and other evidence sufficient to initiate a legitimate alternative verification process, but the employee cannot access the necessary functions without first obtaining the code sent to the missing telephone.

The verification system has ceased serving its intended purpose. It now requires the successful completion of a procedure whose failure is the very reason the individual requires assistance.

This is a logical problem.

A competent systems designer should recognize that the architecture requires an independent recovery pathway. A competent security specialist should recognize that the recovery pathway must be appropriately protected against impersonation and fraudulent access. A competent institution should establish procedures through which authorized employees can employ that pathway and accept responsibility for its execution.

Instead, the unfortunate individual is sometimes instructed to contact customer service, whereupon customer service requests the code sent to the telephone.

The circle is now complete. Somewhere, an ancient Greek philosopher is quietly vomiting into his sandals.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau examined related problems in its 2023 report on chatbots in consumer finance. Among its concerns were customers becoming trapped in repetitive automated interactions when their problems exceeded the systems' capabilities, with access to human assistance remaining difficult or unavailable.[12]

These are not merely irritating conversations. Financial institutions occupy positions of substantial importance in people's lives. An inability to report an unauthorized transaction, resolve an account restriction, correct an erroneous charge, or establish access to one's funds can produce consequences extending considerably beyond the unpleasantness of speaking with a machine.

What is particularly interesting about these circular systems is that each component may be functioning correctly according to its own instructions. The authentication service is protecting the account. The chatbot is following its conversational workflow. The customer-service employee is complying with policy. The website is directing the customer toward the approved recovery procedure.

The individual components may pass every test the organization has established for them.

The system as a whole has failed.

One of the fundamental responsibilities of systems engineering is understanding the behavior of interconnected components, particularly when their interactions produce outcomes different from those anticipated by examining each component independently.

Apparently, a substantial portion of modern civilization could benefit from attending that particular lecture.

## The Password-Protected Human Being

Human identity has acquired an increasingly peculiar relationship with technology.

For much of recorded civilization, people established one another's identities through personal recognition, introductions, documentation, reputation, signatures, and other forms of evidence. These methods had limitations, and the development of reliable digital identity systems has made possible a range of valuable services that would previously have been difficult or impractical to provide.

Modern authentication technologies can protect people against genuinely sophisticated criminal activity. I would prefer that someone possessing my name, a plausible story, and an impressive collection of confidence not be able to empty my bank account simply by smiling at a teller.

But we have also developed systems capable of rendering an actual human being institutionally nonexistent because a six-digit number failed to arrive on a telephone.

There is something rather profound about that development.

The individual's physical existence is insufficient. Their history with the institution may be insufficient. Their ability to provide identification, explain their circumstances, and establish their relationship to the account may be insufficient. Their existence within the institution is contingent upon the successful operation of a chain of technological components whose failure can leave them with no immediate means of proving that they are themselves.

The password has acquired greater institutional authority than the human being it was designed to protect.

And what happens when the equipment upon which authentication depends becomes unavailable? What happens when a person's telephone service is disconnected for nonpayment, their home is destroyed by fire, their wallet is stolen, their email account is compromised, or a natural disaster interrupts communications infrastructure?

A resilient system anticipates these circumstances as part of its ordinary design responsibilities. They are hardly unimaginable events requiring the assistance of an unusually imaginative science-fiction writer.

The National Institute of Standards and Technology recognizes the importance of account recovery within its digital identity guidelines, including circumstances involving the loss or compromise of authentication methods.[13]

The people developing the standards understand that authentication mechanisms can fail.

The interesting question is why so many institutions appear to regard the consequences of that failure as somebody else's problem.

A security system must protect legitimate users against unauthorized access. If its design also makes legitimate access practically impossible under reasonably foreseeable circumstances, the system possesses a significant security and availability problem of its own.

A vault that nobody can open is a remarkably effective vault.

Its usefulness as a banking facility is somewhat less impressive.

## The Machine That Determines Whether I Exist

One of my favorite contemporary technological experiences involves visiting a website I have visited a thousand times before, only to be greeted by the Cloudflare logo and a message informing me that the system is presently determining whether I am human.

Well, this ought to be interesting.

I am sitting in a chair, breathing oxygen, circulating blood, metabolizing breakfast, contemplating the nature of existence, and experiencing a steadily increasing degree of irritation with the machine questioning mine. I possess several decades of accumulated evidence concerning my humanity, including a collection of increasingly uncooperative joints, a digestive system with strong opinions about certain foods, and a remarkable capacity for making decisions I subsequently regret. By virtually every biological, philosophical, and behavioral measure available to civilization, I am demonstrably human.

Nevertheless, a computer would like a moment to think about it.

What makes the experience particularly fascinating is that the machine frequently requires absolutely nothing of me. It conducts its investigation independently, examining characteristics of my browser and its technological environment, apparently consulting whatever collection of electronic tea leaves it considers sufficient to reach a determination concerning my species. Somewhere within this arrangement, a collection of instructions written by human beings is evaluating whether another human being possesses sufficiently human characteristics to be permitted to access information produced by yet more human beings.

And the human being whose existence is presently under investigation has no meaningful participation in the fucking discussion.

I understand the engineering objectives behind these systems. In fact, I employ Cloudflare's infrastructure in my own professional work. Automated malicious traffic, credential attacks, denial-of-service attacks, fraudulent transactions, and other forms of technological misbehavior present genuine operational problems. The ability to distinguish between legitimate traffic and potentially harmful automation is an enormously valuable technological capability.

But somewhere between the engineering department and the little message displayed on my screen, the distinction between evaluating a network request and evaluating a human being became rather spectacularly lost.

The machine cannot establish my humanity. It can establish whether the technological equipment through which I am communicating exhibits characteristics sufficiently consistent with its programmed expectations of human-operated equipment. Those are two profoundly different propositions, particularly when the consequences of confusing them include denying an actual human being access to something they legitimately require.

Cloudflare's own documentation acknowledges that legitimate visitors can become trapped in challenge loops because of network problems, browser configurations, privacy extensions, or detection errors.[14] Consider the peculiar irony of a human being exercising reasonable caution concerning their online privacy, only to discover that the machinery responsible for protecting a website may interpret aspects of that caution as suspicious or find itself unable to complete its verification.

Apparently, exercising human judgment can interfere with our ability to establish that we possess it.

The greater philosophical absurdity lies in the authority we have delegated to these systems. A machine cannot ordinarily be held morally responsible for incorrectly determining that I am unworthy of access. It cannot experience embarrassment upon discovering its mistake, accept responsibility for the time it has wasted, or reconsider the legitimacy of the authority it has been given. Those responsibilities belong to the human beings who designed, purchased, configured, and deployed it. Yet those are precisely the people with whom I am least likely to be afforded an opportunity to communicate when the machine gets it wrong.

We have essentially constructed an electronic doorman capable of refusing entry without possessing the intellectual capacity to understand what a door is, the social capacity to explain its decision, or the professional authority to reconsider it.

There is an additional irony awaiting us as artificial intelligence becomes increasingly integrated into the tools human beings employ to accomplish their work. We are simultaneously developing machines capable of behaving more like humans and security systems designed to exclude machines for behaving too much like humans. Eventually, an artificial intelligence acting legitimately on behalf of a human being may encounter another artificial intelligence responsible for determining whether the first artificial intelligence is sufficiently human to conduct the human being's business.

Meanwhile, the actual human being is sitting in a chair waiting for the two fucking appliances to reach an agreement.

I would suggest that somewhere in our enthusiasm for technological advancement, we have misplaced a rather fundamental understanding of the proper hierarchy of things.

The purpose of security technology is to protect human beings and their legitimate activities. Its authority should extend only as far as necessary to accomplish that objective, and its failures should remain subject to meaningful human review and correction. The determination that a particular network request presents an unacceptable risk may be entirely legitimate. The transformation of that determination into an effectively unchallengeable denial of access deserves considerably more scrutiny.

I am perfectly comfortable with a computer evaluating the security implications of my browser's behavior.

I am considerably less comfortable with a civilization that has become so intellectually complacent that it allows a machine to question my humanity while apparently requiring nobody to remain accountable for its answer.

## Congratulations on Your Downgrade

Lest anybody suspect that my enthusiasm for artificial intelligence has afforded its developers diplomatic immunity from the observations contained in this essay, allow me to introduce a particularly irritating example involving the very technology with which I am developing it.

I use ChatGPT extensively in my professional, intellectual, and creative work. Among its genuinely useful features is the ability to produce substantial bodies of written material within a designated writing block, accompanied by controls for copying the material or opening it in an editor. Until recently, in the interface I was using, those controls remained accessible as I worked my way through the document. Upon reaching its conclusion, having read and evaluated the material, I could copy it or open it for further editing without undertaking an additional expedition through the document itself.

Somebody has apparently decided that this was unnecessarily convenient.

The controls now remain at the beginning of the writing block, requiring me to scroll back through the entire document to perform the very operation I am most likely to want to perform upon reaching its conclusion. In the case of an essay of this length, that represents a rather impressive quantity of unnecessary scrolling.

What fascinates me about this particular example is that OpenAI possesses some of the most sophisticated computational infrastructure in human history. Its researchers are investigating artificial reasoning, developing increasingly capable models, and contemplating technological developments with potentially civilization-altering implications. Somewhere within that extraordinary concentration of intellectual capital, however, the copy button appears to have suffered a setback.

We are pursuing artificial general intelligence while apparently experiencing difficulties maintaining the whereabouts of a fucking button.

The problem may seem comparatively trivial, and in isolation it is. But the significance of such examples lies in their accumulation and in what they reveal about the relationship between software development and the people using the resulting products. A development team can introduce a modification, satisfy its internal objectives, and move on to its next project, while thousands or potentially millions of users inherit a small additional expenditure of effort every time they perform an ordinary task.

The developers may never experience the cumulative consequences of that decision. Their customers will.

I would dearly love to see technology companies adopt a principle borrowed from the Hippocratic tradition: before improving something, establish whether you're about to make it worse. Existing functionality represents accumulated knowledge about the relationship between a tool and its users. Removing or diminishing that functionality should require considerably more justification than the fact that somebody has developed a different way of arranging the interface.

Progress ought to be cumulative. When an improvement in one area introduces an unnecessary deterioration in another, the deterioration deserves to be included in the accounting. Otherwise, we are merely congratulating ourselves on the installation of a new staircase while somebody quietly removes the fucking handrail.

And yes, OpenAI, I am talking to you.

## The Poverty Penalty

The nine-business-day delay in our access to the two thousand dollars involved in the opening chapter of this essay provides a particularly useful illustration of something frequently overlooked in discussions of technological and administrative inconvenience.

The consequences of inconvenience are extraordinarily dependent upon the economic circumstances of the individual experiencing it.

A company maintaining substantial cash reserves may regard a two-thousand-dollar deposit hold as a minor annoyance. Its bills are paid, its employees receive their wages, and its ongoing operations remain unaffected. The owner may barely notice the delay.

For an emerging business, however, two thousand dollars might represent the ability to purchase necessary equipment, obtain insurance, pay a subcontractor, fund a marketing initiative, or meet some other obligation upon which its immediate development depends.

The same administrative decision can produce radically different economic consequences.

According to the Federal Reserve's 2025 Survey of Household Economics and Decisionmaking, published in May 2026, only 63 percent of American adults reported that they would cover a hypothetical four-hundred-dollar emergency expense entirely with cash or its equivalent.[15] The remaining respondents were not necessarily incapable of paying the expense, but they would need other arrangements, potentially involving borrowing, carrying credit-card debt, selling something, or being unable to meet the expense in full.

Against that economic backdrop, consider the effects of delayed access to money, erroneous charges, interrupted benefits, insurance disputes, or technological failures that temporarily prevent people from conducting their financial affairs.

The people least able to absorb the consequences are often those least equipped to navigate the machinery responsible for producing them.

Pew Research Center's analysis of its 2025 survey found that approximately 16 percent of American adults depended upon smartphones for internet access without subscribing to home broadband. Lower-income households were disproportionately represented among people facing limitations in their access to digital infrastructure.[16]

Yet the systems through which we administer increasingly essential aspects of life are frequently designed around assumptions concerning the availability of compatible devices, reliable connections, uninterrupted telephone service, accessible email accounts, digital literacy, and sufficient discretionary time to troubleshoot problems.

The technology industry celebrates the ubiquity of the smartphone. An institution subsequently interprets that ubiquity as permission to construct a system in which ownership of a functioning, compatible smartphone becomes a practical prerequisite to participation.

Owning a smartphone and possessing reliable access to the entire technological ecosystem are considerably different things.

A person working two jobs may not be able to spend an hour on hold during business hours. A person living in a rural community may not have dependable cellular coverage. An elderly person may struggle with an authentication process designed by somebody who has never experienced deteriorating vision or declining manual dexterity. A person with a disability may encounter software whose accessibility features were evidently added during the development team's lunch break.

A wealthy individual can sometimes purchase assistance, substitute one service for another, absorb the financial consequences, or simply instruct an employee to resolve the matter.

Poverty provides fewer escape routes.

The result is a technological poverty penalty: the accumulation of costs, delays, exclusion, and administrative labor disproportionately imposed upon people with the least capacity to absorb them.

I would suggest that any company advertising the accessibility of its technology should be required, at least by its own intellectual conscience, to evaluate that claim from the perspective of somebody whose telephone is broken, whose vehicle has stopped working, whose account contains forty-seven dollars, and whose employer will not permit them to take the afternoon off to explain their existence to a software application.

That person has every bit as much right to participate in society as the executive who approved the application's development.

## Who Owns the Fucking Problem?

There is a particularly interesting adventure awaiting anybody sufficiently determined to identify the person responsible for resolving a failure within a large modern institution.

The customer-service representative explains that the problem originates with the software. The software vendor explains that the institution controls its configuration. The institution explains that the configuration reflects its internal policies. The department responsible for those policies explains that they were developed according to compliance requirements. Compliance points toward federal regulations, and somewhere beneath several hundred pages of regulatory language one discovers that the government may never have required the particular procedure being imposed.

Nevertheless, the procedure is now apparently immutable.

This is one of the great achievements of contemporary institutional architecture: responsibility can be distributed so thoroughly that everybody involved possesses a plausible explanation for why they are not responsible.

The customer, curiously enough, possesses no corresponding luxury. The individual remains responsible for paying their bills, meeting their contractual obligations, correcting their account information, protecting their credentials, complying with the institution's requirements, and absorbing the consequences of whatever malfunction has interrupted their ability to do those things.

The asymmetry is breathtaking.

We have become accustomed to institutions expecting almost absolute accountability from individuals while maintaining organizational structures capable of dissipating their own accountability across departments, contractors, software vendors, and layers of management.

Consider how we might respond if a customer attempted to adopt the same practices.

Imagine informing your mortgage company that your monthly payment had been delayed because your household's accounts payable department was experiencing a synchronization problem with its banking infrastructure. You have escalated the matter internally, but the relevant system administrator is currently unavailable, and your compliance department has advised you that the matter cannot be resolved until the next scheduled software update.

You sincerely apologize for the inconvenience and encourage the mortgage company to submit its complaint through your online portal.

Do let me know how that fucking conversation goes.

The underlying problem is that the customer is expected to operate as an identifiable, accountable economic entity, while the institution is increasingly permitted to operate as a collection of interconnected procedures whose cumulative behavior nobody appears to own.

That is not merely poor customer service.

It is a fundamentally unhealthy distribution of authority and responsibility.

## The Medical Profession Meets the Administrative Profession

The implications become considerably more serious when we move beyond banking and retail into healthcare.

In its 2025 survey of one thousand practicing physicians, released in 2026, the American Medical Association reported that physicians and their staff spent an average of approximately thirteen hours each week completing prior authorization requirements, with an average of around forty requests per physician per week.[17]

The association represents physicians and has a longstanding position on prior authorization reform. Its survey is a report of practitioners' experiences, not a comprehensive independent measurement of every insurer's operations. Nevertheless, the workload those practitioners describe is sufficiently substantial to warrant serious attention.

Thirteen hours per week is an extraordinary quantity of professional capacity to devote to obtaining permission to provide treatments.

Consider the knowledge represented by those hours. Physicians, nurses, medical assistants, administrative personnel, and other healthcare professionals have collectively invested enormous amounts of time, money, and effort developing their abilities. Many entered their professions because they possessed a sincere interest in caring for human beings.

We subsequently place portions of that accumulated capability inside administrative systems through which they must repeatedly establish the legitimacy of doing what they were trained to do.

Technology has made it possible for insurers to process requests, collect information, evaluate claims, and coordinate administrative operations at scales that would previously have been impractical. Those capabilities could help reduce delays and improve coordination between patients, care providers, and insurers.

They can also facilitate the implementation of increasingly elaborate approval requirements, thereby expanding the volume of administrative activity surrounding the delivery of care.

Whether the technology saves labor or creates it depends substantially upon the institutional purpose it is employed to serve.

The healthcare provider has a patient. The patient has a condition. The insurer has an administrative process. The technology makes it possible for the administrative process to become increasingly sophisticated.

It does not automatically make the patient healthier.

One would hope that, somewhere within the expanding technological and commercial architecture of healthcare, somebody remains sufficiently interested in that particular outcome to measure it.

The principles examined in *The Patient Is Not the Product* extend directly into this territory. A medical institution's technological infrastructure should facilitate the relationship between the patient and the people providing care. When the infrastructure becomes a substantial obstacle to that relationship, its sophistication offers rather little consolation to the individual who remains ill.

A highly efficient system for preventing people from receiving things is still a highly efficient system.

We should probably establish what it is preventing them from receiving before congratulating its designers.

## Artificial Intelligence, Natural Stupidity

I find myself particularly fascinated by the contemporary excitement surrounding artificial intelligence because I am actively participating in it.

Expedition Growth Lab is being developed around capabilities that include customer relationship management systems, digital communications, automation, software integration, artificial intelligence, and the improvement of business processes. I spend considerable amounts of my own time working with technologies whose capabilities would have seemed extraordinary only a few years ago.

I embrace these tools because I understand what they can make possible.

I also understand how catastrophically stupid their implementation can become when the people purchasing, developing, and deploying them lose sight of the problems they were intended to solve.

A 2025 study published in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics* examined the introduction of a generative AI assistant among more than five thousand customer-support agents. The researchers found that access to the assistant increased the number of customer issues resolved per hour by approximately 15 percent on average, with substantial differences in outcomes among workers.[18]

That is genuinely interesting. Artificial intelligence assisted human beings in performing their work, helping them access knowledge and respond to customers more effectively. The technology was introduced into a functioning human activity and evaluated partly according to the outcomes it helped people achieve.

Now consider what happens if the executive responsible for interpreting those results arrives at the conclusion that the remaining human beings constitute an unnecessary intermediate expense.

The system that helped employees resolve customer problems becomes a system designed to prevent customers from reaching employees. Its success is evaluated according to the percentage of interactions it contains within automated channels rather than the quality of the resolutions those interactions produce.

Before long, somebody publishes a presentation announcing that the company has automated 80 percent of its customer-service inquiries.

How many were resolved?

How many customers abandoned the attempt?

How many subsequently contacted the company through another channel?

How many accepted an erroneous outcome because they could no longer afford the time required to pursue the correct one?

And how many simply went away?

A corporation can design a metric that rewards the successful disappearance of the customer without establishing whether the customer's problem has disappeared along with them.

Artificial intelligence dramatically expands our technological capacity. It does not independently establish the wisdom of our objectives.

If we instruct increasingly capable systems to minimize institutional expenses without adequately accounting for customer outcomes, they may become remarkably proficient at transferring inconvenience elsewhere. If we reward them for preventing escalation to human employees, they may become remarkably proficient at keeping frustrated customers away from human employees. If we measure the speed with which they terminate conversations, they may become extraordinarily efficient at terminating conversations.

The intelligence of the technology does not redeem the stupidity of the objective.

This is why I find the indiscriminate enthusiasm surrounding AI adoption somewhat exhausting. Too often, people appear to believe that the introduction of artificial intelligence represents an accomplishment in itself. Businesses announce AI initiatives, governments announce AI strategies, and technology vendors attach the letters AI to products with the enthusiasm of medieval merchants attaching fragments of alleged saints to otherwise unremarkable household objects.

I would prefer to know what the fucking thing does, why it does it, whom it serves, what happens when it fails, and who possesses the authority to intervene.

Those are the interesting questions.

The rest is largely a matter of purchasing decisions.

## The House That Silicon Valley Built

Technology companies deserve considerably more scrutiny than they generally receive for their role in constructing the institutional world in which we now find ourselves.

The contemporary technology industry operates within an economic environment that strongly rewards expansion, customer acquisition, recurring revenue, data accumulation, ecosystem dependence, and the successful establishment of products as indispensable components of other businesses' operations.

A software company that produces a genuinely useful product may attract customers because the product helps them accomplish something. As its market develops, the company may discover additional commercial opportunities in managing the surrounding infrastructure, integrating related services, establishing proprietary dependencies, controlling access to accumulated data, and making departure from its ecosystem increasingly expensive.

Eventually, the customer's ability to stop using the product may become considerably less developed than their ability to commence using it.

The Federal Trade Commission's January 2025 staff report examining partnerships between major cloud providers and artificial intelligence developers identified potential competition concerns involving access to computing resources, engineering talent, sensitive commercial information, and the costs associated with switching technology providers.[19]

The underlying relationships are complex, and large-scale partnerships can provide substantial technical and financial benefits. They also raise an important question concerning how much of civilization's future infrastructure we are comfortable placing within a relatively small collection of privately controlled ecosystems.

The issue extends beyond artificial intelligence. Businesses increasingly rely upon third-party systems for communications, payments, customer information, security, inventory, scheduling, identity verification, website infrastructure, accounting, and innumerable other operational functions.

A business owner can spend years developing an otherwise competent enterprise only to discover that a significant portion of its ability to function is contingent upon decisions made by companies with which the business owner possesses virtually no meaningful negotiating relationship.

The vendor changes its pricing. The vendor modifies its software. The vendor discontinues a function. The vendor introduces a new contractual requirement. The vendor experiences an outage.

The business owner remains responsible for delivering whatever was promised to the customer.

This becomes particularly interesting when we consider the extraordinary operational concentration that can develop beneath apparently diverse businesses. Thousands of companies may present themselves to the public as independent enterprises while relying upon overlapping collections of cloud services, security providers, financial processors, software platforms, and communications infrastructure.

Their independence is partially contingent upon the continued operation of the same relatively small collection of machinery.

In July 2024, a defective update distributed by cybersecurity company CrowdStrike caused widespread disruption to Windows-based computer systems. Microsoft estimated that approximately 8.5 million devices were affected, representing less than one percent of the world's Windows machines. Nevertheless, the consequences extended across airlines, healthcare, banking, and other essential services.[20]

The incident was not a demonstration that cybersecurity software is unnecessary. It was a demonstration of how extensively the consequences of a single technical failure can propagate through interconnected infrastructure.

We have spent decades improving the efficiency with which institutions share technological dependencies.

We might want to spend a little more time examining the efficiency with which those dependencies allow institutions to fail together.

There is an enormous difference between an advanced technological civilization and a civilization that has become technologically dependent without developing a corresponding degree of technological resilience.

The former possesses capabilities.

The latter possesses vulnerabilities with exceptionally impressive marketing departments.

## Please Direct All Complaints to Congress

The lawmakers responsible for establishing the legal environment within which these institutions operate deserve a substantial portion of our attention.

Government has legitimate responsibilities concerning consumer protection, fraud, privacy, competition, financial stability, public safety, accessibility, and the prevention of demonstrable harm. Meeting those responsibilities sometimes requires legislation, regulation, enforcement, and administrative oversight.

The interesting question is whether our lawmakers possess sufficient understanding of the systems they regulate, sufficient independence from the industries seeking to influence their decisions, and sufficient interest in the cumulative consequences of their legislation.

A regulation that appears reasonable when examined independently may interact with dozens of other regulations, institutional policies, technological limitations, contractual requirements, and interpretations produced by compliance departments. The resulting operational environment may become vastly more complicated than anything contemplated by the people who approved its individual components.

Each additional requirement arrives with its own justification. Each institution responds to the requirement according to its interpretation of the associated risks. Each technology vendor modifies its products to accommodate the resulting institutional demand. Each modification introduces another opportunity for duplication, confusion, exclusion, or failure.

Eventually, the citizen finds themselves standing at a counter trying to complete a transaction that requires the successful coordination of procedures established by people who have never met one another.

Nobody has undertaken responsibility for the cumulative experience.

Even the government's own machinery recognizes portions of this problem. The Paperwork Reduction Act establishes a framework for examining federal information-collection requirements and their burdens upon the public. A 2025 Government Accountability Office report found opportunities to improve how federal agencies assess administrative burdens associated with benefit programs and how those burdens are incorporated into agency performance management.[21]

The existence of such an oversight framework establishes that administrative burden is sufficiently important to warrant systematic examination.

I would like to see considerably more of that examination applied to the cumulative experience of citizens interacting with government and the private institutions that implement governmental requirements.

We should also be extraordinarily attentive to the role of commercial influence in determining which regulations are imposed, which are resisted, and which are conveniently left unexamined.

Technology companies, financial institutions, insurers, and other industries maintain sophisticated government-relations operations. They possess resources with which to employ lobbyists, prepare policy proposals, commission research, participate in regulatory proceedings, and cultivate relationships with lawmakers.

An ordinary citizen experiencing the consequences of a dysfunctional system may possess little more than a collection of unanswered emails and a gradually deteriorating vocabulary.

These are hardly equivalent positions from which to participate in the development of public policy.

In 2025 and 2026, technology companies and their industry organizations participated actively in debates concerning the regulation of artificial intelligence, including competing approaches to federal standards and the authority of states to establish their own requirements. Some sought national uniformity, others supported particular forms of state regulation, and disagreements emerged over how those frameworks should interact.[22]

There are legitimate reasons to question the practicality of maintaining numerous incompatible regulatory environments for technologies operating across jurisdictional boundaries. There are equally legitimate reasons to question whether eliminating state authority without establishing adequate replacement protections would leave citizens vulnerable to commercially convenient forms of experimentation.

I am interested in who participates in making those decisions, which interests are represented, whose evidence receives serious consideration, and who will be responsible for the consequences when the resulting systems fail.

A corporation is entitled to explain the difficulties it encounters in complying with regulation. It is not entitled to become the principal author of the public's relationship with the industry in which that corporation operates.

Lawmakers represent human beings.

They should occasionally demonstrate sufficient familiarity with their employers to understand how the machinery they authorize actually affects them.

Perhaps every legislative committee considering an essential digital service should be required to spend a week attempting to use that service under deliberately unfavorable but entirely plausible circumstances. Give them an inexpensive telephone, unreliable internet access, a recently changed address, a limited checking-account balance, and a problem that doesn't appear among the automated system's available options.

Then, when the hearing commences, ask them how their week went.

I suspect some rather interesting amendments would emerge from that exercise.

## And Then There's Us

Having distributed a respectable quantity of criticism among technology companies, corporate executives, software vendors, regulators, insurers, financial institutions, and lawmakers, I should probably acknowledge the remaining participants in this extraordinary arrangement.

Us.

We have been enthusiastic participants in the construction of the world we now inhabit. We have embraced the convenience of digital services, surrendered enormous quantities of personal information, encouraged businesses to develop increasingly immediate methods of delivering products, and developed expectations concerning technological availability that would have astonished previous generations.

We have rewarded companies for making purchases effortless while paying comparatively little attention to the terms under which our continued participation is governed.

We have allowed the ability to accomplish something rapidly to become confused with the desirability of accomplishing it through a particular technological arrangement.

When a company introduces a new application, we download it. When it introduces a new requirement, we attempt to satisfy it. When it eliminates a human service channel, we learn the new automated procedure. When the automated procedure fails, we complain to one another, occasionally produce an entertaining social-media post, and subsequently return to attempting to satisfy the requirements of the automated procedure.

Each accommodation helps establish the new arrangement as commercially viable.

There are substantial limits to an individual's ability to refuse. A person cannot necessarily abandon their electric utility, healthcare provider, employer, or government because its technological infrastructure is badly designed. People frequently have little practical choice concerning the institutions through which they must conduct essential aspects of their lives.

That is precisely why this is a matter of collective concern rather than merely an exercise in individual consumer preference.

We must become considerably more demanding about the relationship between technological convenience and human authority. We should question compulsory applications, unnecessary data collection, unreasonable authentication requirements, inaccessible services, and policies that make institutions difficult to hold accountable for their own failures.

And where genuine alternatives exist, we should become more conscious of which businesses we reward.

A small business that answers its telephone, employs people capable of exercising judgment, resolves problems without unnecessary obstacles, and takes responsibility for the consequences of its own mistakes may provide a considerably more advanced customer experience than a much larger organization operating several million dollars' worth of sophisticated technology.

The quality of service cannot be determined by counting the computers involved.

Occasionally, the most technologically advanced decision available to a human being is recognizing that the appropriate moment has arrived to put the fucking computer down.

## The Return of Minerva Mayhem McCoy

Readers of *The Plant That Terrified Civilization* may remember Minerva Mayhem McCoy, our fictional Appalachian custodian of traditional knowledge, possessor of a proper dirt-floored kitchen, and practitioner of the increasingly exotic human activity of paying attention.

I would be rather interested in her assessment of contemporary technological civilization.

Imagine making the journey up the mountain to visit Minerva, settling into a chair near her fire, and explaining that you have come seeking assistance with some ordinary predicament. Minerva listens, considers your circumstances, and determines whether she possesses the knowledge and materials necessary to help you.

She does not require you to download the Minerva Mayhem McCoy Experience application. She does not send an authentication code to the chimney. She does not instruct you to scan a QR code before directing you toward the appropriate shelf of jars.

And should your particular circumstances differ from those she has previously encountered, she is entirely at liberty to contemplate them.

That last capability is perhaps the most technologically interesting thing about Minerva.

She possesses an adaptive understanding of the relationship between her knowledge, her tools, the people who seek her assistance, and the purposes for which those tools are employed. If the handle of a spoon breaks, she acquires another spoon. If a particular material is unavailable, she considers whether another might serve the same purpose. If somebody presents a problem beyond her knowledge, she may know another person possessing the necessary expertise.

Her operating system is distributed across experience, observation, judgment, human relationships, and the physical resources of her environment.

She is also the individual who possesses the authority to determine how those resources are employed.

Minerva is not a comprehensive model for modern medicine, banking, or cybersecurity. Her kitchen cannot administer an international payments network or coordinate the operation of a modern hospital.

Nevertheless, it contains a principle that some of our most sophisticated technological institutions appear to have misplaced.

The person seeking assistance is the reason for the existence of the process.

Everything else is equipment.

Perhaps Silicon Valley should send somebody up the mountain.

## Technology Is Infrastructure. Stewardship Is the Business.

My professional interest in technology has increasingly led me toward a philosophy of systems design that begins with the human relationships and activities the system is intended to support.

A customer relationship management system, for example, should help a business understand and maintain relationships with its customers. Its value is not established by the quantity of information it accumulates, the complexity of its dashboards, or the number of automated messages it can dispatch while its owner sleeps.

Its value is established by what that information and those capabilities enable the business to do for the people it serves.

Similarly, an automated workflow should reduce unnecessary labor, improve coordination, preserve continuity, and make it easier for people to accomplish worthwhile objectives. If the implementation merely relocates administrative work to customers, eliminates access to informed employees, or creates technological dependencies disproportionate to its benefits, it deserves considerably more examination before being described as an improvement.

Technology is infrastructure. Stewardship is the business. Resilience is the objective.

The infrastructure should remain subordinate to the relationship it supports.

That principle informs the work I want Expedition Growth Lab to undertake. A business's technological systems should strengthen its ability to maintain commitments, develop relationships, respond to changing circumstances, preserve knowledge, improve its operations, and support the people whose efforts sustain it.

A technologically advanced business should be capable of remembering what a customer told it yesterday. It should be capable of identifying who owns a problem, determining what has already been done about it, and coordinating the resources necessary to resolve it. Its employees should possess access to information sufficient to exercise informed judgment, alongside authority proportionate to their responsibilities.

When its infrastructure fails, it should possess alternative means of accomplishing essential activities.

And when none of those means is immediately available, somebody should possess both the responsibility and the authority to explain the situation, establish what can reasonably be done, and remain accountable until the matter has been resolved.

This is hardly an extravagant technological ambition.

In fact, much of it represents something businesses accomplished quite competently before technology made it possible to organize their operations in such a way that nobody could accomplish it anymore.

The opportunity before us is to combine the extraordinary capabilities of modern infrastructure with the judgment, adaptability, responsibility, and human relationships that previously made good businesses good.

There is absolutely no reason we should have to choose between the two.

## A Human Balance Sheet

If we intend to become serious about evaluating technological efficiency, I would propose expanding the boundaries of the calculations through which we determine whether an implementation has been successful.

An organization should examine the total human cost associated with accomplishing the activity its system supports.

Consider two hypothetical customer-service arrangements. Under the first, resolving a particular problem requires fifteen minutes of an employee's time and fifteen minutes of the customer's time. Under the second, automation reduces employee involvement to five minutes but increases the customer's required effort to forty-five minutes.

The organization has saved ten minutes of paid employee time. The customer has incurred an additional thirty minutes of effort. The total human labor associated with resolving the problem has increased from thirty minutes to fifty.

On the institution's balance sheet, the new arrangement may look like an improvement.

From the perspective of the human beings collectively involved, it represents a deterioration.

This elementary accounting exercise ought to be incorporated into the evaluation of technological deployments across numerous industries.

How long does it take a customer to accomplish their objective? How frequently do they encounter obstacles? How many attempts are required? How often must they repeat information? What additional labor is performed by employees attempting to resolve failures originating elsewhere in the system? How many people abandon the process, and what happens to them afterward?

These are measurable operational questions.

A business already possesses considerable incentives to understand the cost of its employees' time. Its customers' time should receive a corresponding degree of consideration, particularly when those customers are required to expend it because of decisions made by the business.

Government agencies should evaluate their administrative requirements similarly. A reduction in the cost of processing applications means relatively little if applicants collectively expend substantially more time completing them. A digital benefits system should be evaluated partly according to whether eligible citizens can successfully obtain the benefits, rather than merely the cost of administering the program.

The same principle applies to healthcare, financial services, utilities, education, and every other essential service.

A civilization's economic resources include the hours available within the lives of its inhabitants.

When we waste those hours, we have wasted something that no subsequent technological advancement can manufacture again.

## Build a Door That Opens

The constructive philosophy emerging from all of this is relatively straightforward, although its implementation will require considerable changes in institutional priorities.

Every essential automated system should possess a practical alternative pathway when its normal operation fails. The availability of that pathway should be evaluated under circumstances involving technological outages, accessibility limitations, compromised credentials, lost devices, and unusual but legitimate customer circumstances. A recovery procedure that depends upon the continued availability of the same infrastructure responsible for the failure is not a satisfactory recovery procedure.

Employees should receive the knowledge, training, authority, and institutional support necessary to exercise judgment within appropriate boundaries. Responsible discretion can be accompanied by documentation, supervisory review, security controls, and clear accountability. The important thing is that somebody remains capable of recognizing a legitimate exception and acting upon it.

Institutions should retain ownership of the problems created by their systems. If a customer's account information becomes corrupted during a software migration, the institution should possess the resources to investigate and correct the discrepancy. The individual should not effectively become the project manager responsible for coordinating the institution's internal departments.

Security and privacy requirements should be proportionate to the actual risks involved, with serious consideration given to the consequences of excluding legitimate users. Technology should help establish identity and protect access rather than making people's practical existence contingent upon an unnecessarily fragile chain of electronic permissions.

The software itself should be developed with the expectation that people will occasionally disagree with its conclusions. Automated decisions affecting essential services, finances, employment, healthcare, or other consequential areas of life should be accompanied by practical means of obtaining explanations, correcting errors, and securing appropriate human review.

These are not particularly revolutionary engineering principles. Versions of them already appear in accessibility standards, human-factors research, information-security frameworks, reliability engineering, and contemporary AI risk-management guidance.[23]

The revolution would consist of treating them as essential to the commercial and civic value of a system rather than as subordinate considerations to be addressed whenever their inclusion does not interfere excessively with a company's preferred financial objectives.

When an engineer designs a building, we expect some consideration of what happens when the normal means of entering or leaving it becomes unavailable. Fire exits are rather inconvenient from the perspective of somebody who would prefer to maximize the rentable floor area.

We nevertheless regard them as legitimate features of a properly designed structure.

The digital architecture of essential services deserves an equivalent philosophy.

Build a door that opens when the electronic lock fails. Establish procedures that allow legitimate users to escape circular administrative requirements. Give the people responsible for helping others the authority necessary to provide that help.

And for Christ's sake, stop putting the emergency exit on the other side of the malfunctioning turnstile.

## The Machinery of Common Authority

The broader philosophy underlying Common Authority concerns the relationship between human beings and the institutions they establish to serve collective purposes.

Institutions acquire authority because people delegate responsibility to them. Governments, financial systems, healthcare organizations, regulatory bodies, and technological infrastructures exist because human beings have developed arrangements through which certain activities can be coordinated more effectively than they could be accomplished independently.

That delegated authority should remain visible, comprehensible, appropriately limited, and answerable to the people whose lives it affects.

Technology introduces an increasingly important dimension to this relationship because it can make the exercise of institutional authority less visible. A human decision becomes a software rule. The software rule becomes an automated workflow. The workflow becomes a commercial service. The service becomes an apparently unavoidable feature of ordinary existence.

Eventually, the individual encounters the consequences of a decision without encountering anybody capable of explaining who made it, why it was made, or how it might be changed.

This is a significant civic problem.

A citizen should be able to distinguish between a legal obligation, an institutional policy, a commercial preference, and a technological limitation. Those categories have become remarkably entangled, allowing organizations to present choices made for their own convenience as though they were requirements imposed by some authority residing beyond the reach of human inquiry.

The computer becomes a conveniently impersonal representative of decisions that human beings remain responsible for making.

We should reject that arrangement.

The fact that a decision has been automated does not dissolve the responsibility of the people and institutions that authorized it. The fact that a procedure has been implemented in software does not elevate the procedure beyond legitimate criticism. The fact that technological infrastructure has become indispensable to a particular institution does not entitle that infrastructure to acquire authority over the human beings the institution exists to serve.

Among the fundamental principles we ought to establish is the right of a human being to challenge a machine's decision without first having to satisfy the machine whose decision is being challenged. That principle should extend throughout the digital architecture of civilization, from the financial institution safeguarding a person's savings to the electronic doorman deciding whether they may enter a website.

This is particularly important as artificial intelligence becomes increasingly integrated into the decisions governing our lives.

We are developing systems capable of producing extraordinarily sophisticated analyses, recommendations, predictions, and responses. Their capabilities may substantially exceed those of individual human beings in numerous areas. Their potential contributions to medicine, science, engineering, education, agriculture, and the understanding of our own existence are immensely exciting.

What we have yet to demonstrate convincingly is our capacity to deploy those capabilities with a corresponding degree of wisdom.

Before we become particularly enthusiastic about delegating more authority to artificial intelligence, perhaps we should demonstrate that we have learned to exercise the authority already available to us.

The acquisition of intelligence, whether natural or artificial, is of comparatively limited value to a civilization that remains unwilling to accept responsibility for what it does with it.

## What We Were Supposed to Be Saving

I find myself returning to that little credit union on the day before Thanksgiving.

There were human beings sitting around a desk, attempting to accomplish an ordinary commercial transaction. The people involved possessed the knowledge necessary to understand what we were trying to accomplish. We had documentation, communication devices, financial instruments, functioning computers, established institutions, and access to technological capabilities that would have seemed almost inconceivable to earlier generations.

What we lacked was a sufficiently dependable pathway through which those capabilities could be coordinated to accomplish the immediate objective.

And as irritating as that experience was, the money was not ultimately the most interesting thing about it.

It was the afternoon.

The afternoon belonged to the people sitting around that desk. It belonged to the employees working within the available infrastructure, to Kendra, to me, and to the other human beings attempting to accomplish their own objectives through similar arrangements.

We could have been building things, learning things, developing our businesses, preparing for Thanksgiving, helping other people, or simply enjoying a few hours of the finite and rather remarkable experience of being alive.

Instead, portions of our lives were consumed attempting to satisfy the requirements of machinery whose existence was principally justified by its ability to facilitate precisely the activities it was obstructing.

How many such afternoons have we collectively surrendered?

How many hours have disappeared into automated telephone systems, password recovery procedures, duplicated paperwork, subscription disputes, failed applications, insurance authorizations, and efforts to establish facts that the relevant institutions already possess?

How much human intelligence has been diverted from productive and creative activity toward the maintenance of unnecessarily complicated relationships with institutions?

And how much of that effort have we allowed ourselves to regard as an unavoidable consequence of progress?

I want technology to continue advancing. I want extraordinary developments in artificial intelligence, renewable energy, medicine, communications, agriculture, manufacturing, and our understanding of the universe. I want people to develop things that neither John Maynard Keynes nor Minerva Mayhem McCoy could have imagined.

But I also want the people developing those things to remember what we were supposed to be advancing toward.

The objective was never to maximize the amount of technological infrastructure human beings interact with during the course of their lives. It was to expand their capabilities, relieve unnecessary burdens, improve their circumstances, and give them greater opportunities to determine what they wished to do with those lives.

We developed tools because there were things we wanted to accomplish. We developed institutions because there were things we could accomplish more effectively together. We developed technology because we possessed the imagination to recognize that the limitations of our existing circumstances did not necessarily have to remain the limitations of our future.

Those are beautiful characteristics of humanity.

It would be a terrible shame to allow the infrastructure produced by those characteristics to gradually extinguish them.

There is a rather wonderful image in Keynes's essay of a future humanity confronting the question of how to live wisely and enjoyably once technological and economic development has relieved much of the burden of material necessity.

Nearly a century later, I think that remains an enormously worthwhile objective. I would like us to develop a civilization in which technological progress returns meaningful quantities of time, autonomy, security, and opportunity to the people whose ingenuity made it possible.

I would like our children and grandchildren to inherit extraordinary tools without inheriting the expectation that their lives must be organized around satisfying the administrative requirements of those tools.

I would like a competent human being to be able to recognize an absurdity, correct it, and get on with their afternoon.

Technology was supposed to make our lives easier because our lives were supposed to be about something more interesting than technology.

Perhaps that is the instruction we should have included in the fucking owner's manual.
