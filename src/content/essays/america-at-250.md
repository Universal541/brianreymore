###The *Real* 250th

There is a particular kind of heartbreak reserved for things you love.

Not things you merely enjoy.

Not things you admire from a distance.

Things that helped shape you.

Things that became part of you.

Things whose victories feel personal and whose failures feel strangely intimate.

That is how I feel about America.

Not because I believe America is perfect.

Good God, no.

A person would have to know almost nothing about American history to believe that.

The nation was born in contradiction. It declared that all men were created equal while permitting slavery. It championed liberty while denying it to many. It spoke the language of universal rights while reserving those rights unevenly and often hypocritically. The American story has always been a collision between extraordinary ideals and deeply imperfect human beings.

That tension is not a flaw in the story.

It *is* the story.

For two hundred and fifty years, generation after generation has inherited the same unfinished argument.

What kind of nation are we trying to become?

The founders argued about it.

The abolitionists argued about it.

The suffragists argued about it.

The labor organizers argued about it.

The civil rights movement argued about it.

Every generation has wrestled with the distance between our principles and our practice.

And somehow, despite our failures, despite our hypocrisy, despite our moments of breathtaking cruelty and staggering injustice, the argument itself endured.

That may be America's greatest achievement.

Not perfection.

Correction.

The stubborn belief that tomorrow can be better than today.

The radical notion that citizens possess both the right and the responsibility to improve the republic they inherited.

The understanding that patriotism is not obedience.

Patriotism is stewardship.

Which brings us to the uncomfortable part.

Because as America turns 250 years old, I find myself increasingly unsure whether we still understand the difference.

We celebrate freedom while treating citizenship as a spectator sport.

We celebrate opportunity while constructing systems that increasingly reward extraction over contribution.

We celebrate innovation while confusing disruption with wisdom.

We celebrate wealth while struggling to remember why wealth was supposed to matter in the first place.

Most troubling of all, we seem to have forgotten a truth so obvious that previous generations would have considered it self-evident:

A republic cannot survive on laws alone.

It survives on trust.

It survives on restraint.

It survives on good faith.

It survives because enough people choose to honor the spirit of the agreement rather than searching endlessly for loopholes in its wording.

The Constitution was never intended to function as an instruction manual for clever people determined to discover the maximum amount of selfishness legally permissible.

It was designed for citizens.

And the difference is a profound one.

One that may explain far more about our current moment than any election, political party, news network, social media platform, or public figure ever could.

Because the great crisis facing America at 250 is not merely political.

It is cultural.

It is civic.

It is moral.

It is the slow erosion of the unwritten agreements that make self-government possible.

The founders worried about this constantly.

Their concern ran deeper than government itself.

They distrusted human nature.

They understood something we have spent the last century trying desperately to forget:

Every freedom eventually depends upon character.

Every institution eventually depends upon trust.

Every republic eventually depends upon citizens willing to place something larger than themselves above their immediate self-interest.

History offers countless examples of what happens when that willingness disappears.

The question facing us now is whether America is drifting toward the same fate.

And if so, whether we still possess the courage to change course.

## The Most Dangerous Idea in Human History

It is easy to forget how absurd America once seemed.

Not merely unlikely.

Absurd.

For most of human history, power worked a certain way. Kings ruled because they were kings. Nobles ruled because they were nobles. Priests interpreted reality on behalf of everyone else. Empires expanded. Subjects obeyed. Bloodlines determined destiny.

That was the normal arrangement.

That was history.

Then a collection of farmers, merchants, lawyers, printers, soldiers, smugglers, philosophers, and professional troublemakers decided they had a better idea.

It is difficult to overstate how radical that idea was.

The Declaration of Independence is so familiar to modern Americans that many no longer hear its audacity.

Governments derive their just powers from the consent of the governed.

Not from God.

Not from a king.

Not from inherited privilege.

From the people.

Today, those words feel obvious.

In 1776, they bordered on insanity.

The founders were not saints.

Washington owned slaves.

Jefferson's contradictions could fill volumes.

Hamilton was brilliant and deeply flawed.

Adams could be stubborn enough to start an argument in an empty room.

Franklin spent much of his life proving that genius and mischief are often roommates.

These men disagreed constantly.

They compromised.

They failed.

They succeeded.

They left unfinished work for every generation that followed.

Yet they shared one extraordinary conviction.

They believed ordinary people could govern themselves.

No king.

No emperor.

No hereditary aristocracy.

No permanent ruling class.

Just citizens.

The idea was fragile.

Still is.

Because self-government is among the most unnatural political arrangements ever attempted.

It requires people to exercise restraint when they possess power.

It requires leaders to surrender authority when the law demands it.

It requires citizens to think beyond themselves.

It requires a population willing to accept losing elections without abandoning the republic.

Most importantly, it requires trust.

Not blind trust.

Earned trust.

The trust necessary for millions of strangers to participate in a shared civic project without tearing one another apart.

The founders understood this.

Which is why they worried incessantly about virtue.

Modern Americans often misunderstand the word.

We hear "virtue" and imagine moralizing sermons or religious lectures.

That is not what they meant.

They meant character, duty, stewardship and self-restraint.

The capacity to choose what is right even when what is profitable sits temptingly nearby.

The capacity to think in generations rather than quarters.

The capacity to place the republic above personal ambition.

They feared corruption for the same reason engineers fear structural cracks.

Not that a few cracks cause collapse, but enough cracks eventually do.

And from the very beginning, the cracks were there.

The republic expanded across a continent.

It generated extraordinary wealth.

It attracted dreamers, builders, inventors, immigrants, laborers, and entrepreneurs from every corner of the globe.

It also produced speculators, monopolists, demagogues, profiteers, and opportunists.

The same freedom that allowed greatness allowed exploitation.

The same openness that attracted visionaries attracted grifters.

The same system that rewarded innovation occasionally rewarded greed.

America never escaped this tension.

It simply became powerful enough to survive it.

For a while.

The story we often tell ourselves is that America became great because it was uniquely virtuous.

The truth is both more complicated and more inspiring.

America became great because each generation fought to drag it closer to its own ideals.

Perfection wasn't achieved.

But the argument continued.

Citizens continued showing up.

Enough people believed the promise was worth defending, even when the reality fell painfully short of it.

That belief built railroads.

It built universities.

It built industries.

It built movements.

It built communities.

It built a nation that became, for hundreds of millions of people around the world, something bordering on a myth.

The place where tomorrow might be better than yesterday.

The place where your children might live better than you did.

The place where reinvention remained possible.

The place where history's ceiling could become your floor.

For generations, that promise drew people across oceans.

America was never perfect.

What made it remarkable was that it remained possible.

And that distinction may be the most important one in the entire story.

## The Builders, The Barons, and the Bastards

One of the most persistent myths in American history is that the nation's problems are somehow new.

They aren't.

The names change.

The technologies change.

The scale changes.

The pattern remains remarkably consistent.

America has always produced builders.

America has always produced opportunists.

Quite often, they have been the same people.

The nineteenth century gave us some of the most extraordinary industrial growth the world had ever witnessed. Railroads stitched together a continent. Steel transformed cities. Electricity reshaped daily life. Factories produced goods at scales previously unimaginable. Entire industries emerged almost overnight.

America was becoming an economic superpower.

It was also becoming a playground for men who discovered that power accumulates far more efficiently than wealth.

Cornelius Vanderbilt.

John D. Rockefeller.

Andrew Carnegie.

J.P. Morgan.

Even now, those names carry a strange duality.

Without them, America might not have industrialized as quickly as it did.

Because of them, entire sectors of the economy became concentrated in the hands of a few extraordinarily powerful individuals.

Both statements are true.

The builders built.

The barons consolidated.

The republic prospered.

The republic suffered.

History, unfortunately, is rarely courteous enough to provide cleaner villains.

Rockefeller helped create one of the most efficient business operations in human history.

He also perfected many of the tactics that allowed monopolies to crush competitors.

Carnegie helped build the industrial backbone of modern America.

His factories also became symbols of labor exploitation and violent industrial conflict.

Morgan helped stabilize financial markets during moments of panic.

He also demonstrated how dangerous it can become when private power begins rivaling public authority.

The lesson is not that wealth is evil.

The lesson is that concentrated power—whether economic, political, military, technological, or cultural—eventually begins behaving in predictable ways.

It seeks preservation.

It seeks expansion.

It seeks influence.

It seeks immunity.

The individuals involved may possess admirable qualities.

The incentives remain the same.

That is why republics require citizens.

Not subjects.

Not fans.

Citizens.

Someone must eventually ask uncomfortable questions.

Someone must eventually stand in front of concentrated power and say:

*"Enough."*

Fortunately, America has never lacked for troublemakers.

When monopolies became too large, Theodore Roosevelt came looking for a fight.

When industrial workers became disposable, labor organizers came looking for a fight.

When segregation became institutionalized, civil rights leaders came looking for a fight.

When political machines became too corrupt, reformers came looking for a fight.

Again and again, American history reveals the same uncomfortable truth.

Progress did not happen because powerful people suddenly developed a conscience.

Progress happened because citizens became impossible to ignore.

There's a difference that matters here.

Because it reminds us that America's greatest achievements rarely emerged from comfort.

They emerged from conflict.

Not violence.

Conflict.

The productive friction created when citizens decide that the gap between our ideals and our reality has become too large to tolerate.

Frederick Douglass was not celebrated by the establishment of his day.

Ida B. Wells was not celebrated by the establishment of her day.

Susan B. Anthony was not celebrated by the establishment of her day.

Martin Luther King Jr. was not celebrated by the establishment of his day.

The people we now place in history books often spent their lives being told they were dangerous, disruptive, unrealistic, radical, or unpatriotic.

History eventually rendered a different verdict.

Which should make every generation a little more cautious about assuming today's critics are automatically wrong.

Or that today's elites are automatically right.

Because power has a peculiar tendency to convince itself that its own interests are synonymous with the public good.

History repeatedly demonstrates otherwise.

And nowhere is that lesson more visible than in the story of the last half-century.

Because sometime around the late twentieth century, America began drifting into a different relationship with power.

Not suddenly.

Gradually.

Quietly.

One merger.

One loophole.

One lobbying effort.

One deregulation package.

One campaign contribution.

One television network.

One algorithm.

One quarterly earnings report at a time.

The changes rarely arrived with marching bands or emergency broadcasts.

Most appeared technical.

A regulatory adjustment here.

A tax provision there.

A court decision that seemed obscure at the time.

The repeal of Glass-Steagall helped dismantle barriers that had separated commercial and investment banking for decades. Citizens United accelerated the influence of money in politics by dramatically expanding the role of independent political spending. Waves of media consolidation placed increasing portions of the national conversation into fewer and fewer corporate hands. Lobbyists and regulators moved through the same revolving doors, often serving both public and private interests during the course of a single career.

None of these developments individually transformed America.

Together, they altered incentives.

And incentives, far more than speeches, shape behavior.

The executive who prioritizes quarterly earnings over long-term stability is responding to incentives.

The politician who spends more time fundraising than governing is responding to incentives.

The media company that discovers outrage outperforms nuance is responding to incentives.

The technology platform that learns anger generates engagement is responding to incentives.

The tragedy is that incentives are often invisible until their consequences become impossible to ignore.

The republic did not wake up one morning transformed.

It was nudged.

Then nudged again.

Then nudged again.

Until many Americans could no longer remember where the original path had been.

## When the Scoreboard Changed

Most Americans can feel that something changed.

Ask them when, and the answers vary wildly.

Some point to the 1960s.

Others point to the Reagan years.

Others to the end of the Cold War.

Some blame the internet.

Some blame globalization.

Some blame social media.

Most of them are describing symptoms.

The deeper shift was simpler.

The scoreboard changed.

For much of American history, wealth was respected.

It was never the sole measure of success.

A wealthy businessman might sit beside a respected teacher, a decorated veteran, a judge, a minister, a scientist, a physician, or a civic leader and understand that his bank account was not the only currency that mattered.

Prestige came from many places.

Service mattered.

Duty mattered.

Character mattered.

Contribution mattered.

Then something happened.

Slowly at first.

Then all at once.

We began collapsing every measure of success into a single number.

Money.

Not prosperity.

Money.

Not value.

Money.

Not contribution.

Money.

And once that happened, the incentives began reshaping everything around them.

In 1970, the economist Milton Friedman published a famous argument asserting that the primary social responsibility of business was to increase profits for shareholders.

The idea was not entirely new.

What was new was how completely corporate America eventually embraced it.

By the 1980s, executives were increasingly judged not by the long-term health of the companies they managed but by quarterly performance. Wall Street rewarded immediate gains. Boards rewarded stock prices. Financial markets rewarded efficiencies, whether or not those efficiencies strengthened the communities beneath them.

Then came men like Jack Welch.

To many investors, Welch was a genius.

To many workers, he became the face of a new philosophy.

Factories closed.

Jobs moved overseas.

Communities hollowed out.

Stock prices rose.

The scoreboard said victory.

The town left behind saw something else.

Again, the point is not that one man caused everything.

The point is that an entire culture began internalizing a new lesson.

If profits rise, almost anything can be justified.

The logic spread.

Healthcare became increasingly financialized.

Higher education became increasingly financialized.

Housing became increasingly financialized.

Even basic necessities began attracting investors searching for opportunities to extract additional value.

The language changed.

The incentives remained.

Patients became revenue streams.

Students became debt holders.

Homes became assets.

Citizens became consumers.

The language shifted almost imperceptibly.

Students became customers.

Patients became revenue streams.

Workers became labor costs.

Homes became investment vehicles.

Citizens became market segments.

The terminology sounded efficient.

The consequences felt increasingly human.

And consumers, unlike citizens, are not expected to think about the common good.

They are expected to buy things.

The transformation was subtle enough that many people never noticed it happening.

The consequences were not.

Entire regions lost industries.

Life expectancy began flattening.

Deaths of despair increased.

Communities weakened.

Trust declined.

None of these concerns are new.

In fact, some were being voiced generations ago.

When President Dwight Eisenhower left office in 1961, he issued one of the most remarkable warnings ever delivered by an American president. A five-star general who had commanded Allied forces in Europe during World War II, Eisenhower was hardly a pacifist. He understood military power as well as any leader in modern history.

Yet in his farewell address, he warned Americans about what he called the "military-industrial complex."

His warning remains striking today:
"We must guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex."

His concern was not merely military spending.

It was influence.

The possibility that powerful institutions, acting in their own interests, might acquire enough economic and political leverage to shape public policy in ways increasingly detached from the public good.

More than sixty years later, the warning feels less like a historical curiosity and more like a recurring American challenge.

Defense contractors.

Pharmaceutical companies.

Technology giants.

Financial institutions.

Media conglomerates.

The industry changes.

The underlying tension remains remarkably familiar.

How much power can any institution accumulate before stewardship gives way to self-preservation?

Every generation of Americans eventually confronts that question.

Every generation answers it differently.

And while all of this was occurring, another revolution arrived.

One even more powerful.

One whose architects would eventually become some of the most influential individuals in human history.

The digital revolution promised connection.

What it discovered was attention.

And once attention became the product, the game changed again.

Because unlike oil, steel, railroads, or manufacturing, attention extraction scales infinitely.

The more distracted the population became, the more profitable the system became.

The more emotionally activated citizens became, the longer they stayed engaged.

The longer they stayed engaged, the more advertisements could be sold.

The incentives were invisible.

The effects were not.

Algorithms did not create tribalism.

They rewarded it.

Algorithms did not invent outrage.

They monetized it.

Algorithms did not manufacture division.

They discovered that division performed exceptionally well.

The old robber barons controlled railroads.

The new barons controlled attention.

History had changed costumes.

The play remained remarkably familiar.

And once again, the central question facing the republic was not whether wealth existed.

Not whether innovation existed.

Not whether powerful people existed.

The question was whether citizens remained capable of placing boundaries around power before power forgot where the boundaries belonged.

## The Republic in the Mirror

There is a temptation that appears in every generation.

It is understandable.

It is human.

It is also dangerous.

The temptation is to imagine that the nation's problems exist somewhere else.

In Washington.

In boardrooms.

In Silicon Valley.

In newsrooms.

In universities.

In corporations.

In political parties.

In institutions.

Anywhere but the mirror.

To be clear, many institutions deserve criticism.

Some deserve outright condemnation.

Powerful people should be held accountable.

Powerful organizations should be scrutinized.

Corruption should be exposed.

Abuses should be challenged.

That is not merely a right of citizenship.

It is an obligation.

But eventually an uncomfortable question appears.

Who allowed all of this?

Not once.

Repeatedly.

Generation after generation.

Who tolerated the lobbying?

Who rewarded the demagoguery?

Who consumed the outrage?

Who elected the opportunists?

Who clicked the headlines?

Who traded civic engagement for entertainment?

Who confused citizenship with spectatorship?

The answer is painful.

The answer is us.

Not all at once.

Not equally.

Not individually.

Collectively.

Because a republic is not a machine that operates independently of its citizens.

A republic is its citizens.

That was the entire point.

The founders did not create a kingdom.

They did not create an empire.

They did not create a system where all responsibility could be outsourced to distant rulers while ordinary people focused exclusively on private concerns.

They created something far more demanding.

A self-governing society.

Which sounds wonderful until one recognizes what it requires.

Self-government requires citizens who are willing to govern themselves.

Not merely their neighbors.

Themselves.

Their impulses.

Their appetites.

Their tribal instincts.

Their desire for simple answers.

Their attraction to charismatic strongmen.

Their tendency to believe comforting lies over uncomfortable truths.

The Constitution can create guardrails.

It cannot create maturity.

The courts can enforce laws.

They cannot manufacture wisdom.

The market can create wealth.

It cannot create character.

Those things must come from somewhere else.

And for much of American history, they did.

Imperfectly.

Unevenly.

But they did.

Families taught responsibility.

Communities reinforced expectations.

Religious institutions, civic organizations, unions, local newspapers, schools, service clubs, neighborhoods, and social networks all helped create something increasingly rare in modern America:

Citizens.

People who understood that freedom was not merely permission.

Freedom was responsibility.

The two were inseparable.

The right to speak implied a responsibility to think.

The right to vote implied a responsibility to learn.

The right to criticize implied a responsibility to participate.

The right to pursue happiness implied a responsibility not to destroy the conditions that made happiness possible for everyone else.

Somewhere along the way, we began forgetting this.

Gradually.

Then rapidly.

We became audiences.

Consumers.

Fans.

Followers.

Subscribers.

Viewers.

Users.

Customers.

Everything except citizens.

Politics became entertainment.

News became content.

Outrage became a business model.

Attention became a commodity.

And while we argued about teams, someone quietly purchased the stadium.

That sentence may explain more about modern America than an entire shelf of political science textbooks.

Because the greatest trick ever played on the American public was convincing us that the most important battle was between left and right.

Those differences aren't unimportant.

They just distract from another divide growing quietly in the background.

The divide between stewardship and extraction.

Between citizenship and consumption.

Between people building something and people harvesting it.

The old arguments remain.

But the landscape beneath them has shifted.

And while citizens have been busy fighting one another, enormous institutions have become larger, wealthier, more influential, and less accountable than previous generations would have tolerated for a second.

Not because they seized power.

Because power was surrendered.

One distracted citizen at a time.

One neglected school board election at a time.

One ignored city council meeting at a time.

One abandoned civic responsibility at a time.

The republic was not stolen.

The republic was neglected.

There is a difference.

And if there is any hope buried within this story, it lives there.

Because what is stolen must be recovered.

What is neglected can be rebuilt.

.....if enough Americans still remember how.

## The Spirit of the Contract

The older I get, the less convinced I am that America's greatest problem is political.

Politics is where the symptoms appear.

Politics is where the fever shows up.

The disease often begins somewhere else.

It begins when people stop honoring the spirit of the agreement.

Every society operates on two kinds of rules.

The written ones.

And the unwritten ones.

The written rules are easy to identify. They fill constitutions, statutes, contracts, regulations, court decisions, and policy manuals. Entire professions exist to interpret them. Entire industries exist to navigate them.

The unwritten rules are harder to see.

They live in expectations.

Norms.

Customs.

Traditions.

Shared understandings.

They exist in the space between what is legal and what is right.

For most of human history, those unwritten rules carried an astonishing amount of weight.

A handshake meant something.

A promise meant something.

A reputation meant something.

That certainly wasn't because people were morally superior.

It was because trust was valuable.

Communities were smaller.

Consequences were closer.

People understood that if everybody behaved in bad faith, life became exhausting.

The written rules were never intended to carry the entire burden of civilization.

They were supposed to support a culture that already possessed a basic commitment to good faith.

Somewhere along the way, we began losing sight of that last critical fact.

Not all at once.

Gradually.

One loophole at a time.

One technicality at a time.

One clever workaround at a time.

One "well, actually..." at a time.

We became a society increasingly fascinated by the difference between what was legal and what was honorable.

Then we started treating the gap as an opportunity.

The result is visible everywhere.

Contracts grow longer.

Regulations grow thicker.

Disclosures grow more absurd.

Terms of service become novels.

Every transaction requires another layer of protection because somebody, somewhere, proved willing to exploit the absence of it.

The paperwork is not the disease.

The paperwork is the scar tissue.

The scar tissue formed because trust became scarce.

And trust became scarce because too many people discovered they could profit from violating it.

The old moral lesson was simple:

Just because you can does not mean you should.

The modern lesson often sounds more like:

If you can get away with it, why wouldn't you?

That shift may explain more about our current moment than any election ever could.

Because once enough people begin operating according to the second principle, everything becomes more complicated.

Every agreement requires enforcement.

Every transaction requires verification.

Every institution requires monitoring.

Every relationship becomes slightly more adversarial.

Civilization begins spending more and more energy defending itself from bad actors rather than creating opportunities for good ones.

The tragedy is that many of the people responsible for this transformation consider themselves exceptionally clever.

In a narrow sense, they often are.

They found the loophole.

They exploited the asymmetry.

They optimized the system.

They maximized the return.

They won.

At least according to the scoreboard.

What they rarely acknowledge is the bill they left for everyone else.

The additional bureaucracy.

The additional regulation.

The additional distrust.

The additional complexity.

The additional cynicism.

The additional burden placed upon ordinary people simply trying to navigate daily life.

A society can survive a certain amount of this.

Eventually, however, the cost becomes visible.

People stop trusting institutions.

People stop trusting leaders.

People stop trusting businesses.

People stop trusting one another.

And once trust begins disappearing, republics become very difficult places to maintain.

This is one reason I find myself thinking about organizations that many modern Americans barely notice anymore.

The Rotary Club.

The Lions.

The Elks.

The local lodge.

The volunteer fire department.

The church committee.

The Little League board.

The neighborhood association.

None of them were perfect.

Many struggled to adapt.

Some deserved criticism.

That is not the point.

The point is that they taught something increasingly rare.

Participation.

Responsibility.

Service.

They were places where citizens practiced citizenship before they were asked to practice politics.

Places where people learned how to work with neighbors they did not entirely agree with.

Places where they learned that community is not something consumed.

It is something built.

Many of those institutions have weakened.

Some have disappeared entirely.

Their decline is not the sole cause of our current troubles.

But it may be one of the canaries in the coal mine.

Because a republic ultimately depends upon citizens who know how to belong to something larger than themselves.

And when belonging disappears, other things rush in to fill the vacuum.

Brands, parties, influencers, algorithms, tribes and spectacles.

The substitutes provide identity.

They rarely provide stewardship.

And stewardship, more than anything else, is what the American experiment requires now.

Not more outrage.

Not more slogans.

Not more teams.

Stewardship.

The willingness to inherit something imperfect and leave it better than we found it.

That is the contract.

Always has been.

Here's the question: Do enough of us still remember signing it?

### The Republic in Miniature

There are moments when the health of a republic becomes visible in places no pollster ever measures.

Not in Congress.

Not in the stock market.

Not in a presidential approval rating.

In a lodge hall.

A volunteer fire station.

A church basement.

A Little League concession stand.

A place where ordinary people quietly perform the unglamorous work required to hold a community together.

Years ago, a small group of people fought to save a struggling Eagles aerie on the Oregon coast.

The charter had already been suspended.

The future was uncertain.

The building needed work.

The books needed work.

The technology needed work.

The membership needed work.

Everything needed work.

So a handful of people stepped forward.

Not for money. Not for recognition. Not because anybody promised them a reward.

Because somebody had to.

They cooked meals.

Worked the bar.

Balanced the books.

Maintained the building.

Served as officers.

Ran events.

Covered shifts.

Solved problems.

Filled vacancies.

Did whatever needed doing because organizations, like republics, survive only when enough people are willing to carry responsibilities that nobody is forcing them to carry.

For five years they held the place together with determination, duct tape, and good intentions.

Against the odds, they succeeded.

The charter was restored.

The organization survived.

And then something happened that I find impossible to forget.

Almost nobody stepped forward to relieve them.

The people carrying the weight had become exhausted.

The people benefiting from the effort had become accustomed to watching.

Eventually the stewards ran out of energy.

The paperwork had been restored.

The participation had not.

I think about that story often because it feels less like the story of a lodge and more like the story of modern citizenship.

The challenge facing America today is not that nobody cares.

The challenge is that too few people are carrying too much of the load.

The same names appear on the volunteer lists.

The same people coach the teams.

Run the fundraisers.

Organize the events.

Serve on the boards.

Attend the meetings.

Write the checks.

Fix the roof.

Sweep the floor.

Then one day they look around the room and discover they have become the room.

The bench behind them is empty.

No republic collapses because citizens care too much.

The greater danger has always been that too few care enough.

And if there is a lesson buried inside that little Eagles aerie on the Oregon coast, it may be this:

The American experiment has never depended primarily on presidents, billionaires, celebrities, generals, corporations, or political parties.

It depends upon ordinary people voluntarily accepting responsibility for something larger than themselves.

The moment enough people stop doing that, no Constitution on Earth can save the republic.

The paperwork remains.

The institutions remain.

The elections remain.

But the spirit that made them work begins slipping quietly out the back door.

And by the time anyone notices, it is often already gone.

## The Promise and the Betrayal

Every nation survives on stories.

Not myths.

Stories.

The distinction matters.

A myth asks people to ignore reality.

A story helps people understand it.

For most of American history, the central story was remarkably simple.

Work hard.

Contribute.

Play by the rules.

Take responsibility.

Leave things better than you found them.

Your children will likely inherit a life better than your own.

The promise was never universally true.

Millions of Americans were denied access to it.

Others struggled mightily to achieve it.

Entire communities were excluded from it for generations.

The story contained contradictions from the beginning.

Yet even those contradictions carried a strange power.

Because the promise existed.

People could point to it.

Argue for it.

Demand inclusion within it.

The abolitionist could invoke it.

The suffragist could invoke it.

The labor organizer could invoke it.

The civil rights movement could invoke it.

America's greatest reformers rarely argued against the promise.

They argued that the nation was failing to honor it.

That argument changed history.

The promise became a measuring stick.

A standard.

An aspiration.

A challenge.

And for a long time, despite all our flaws, many Americans believed the gap between reality and the promise was narrowing.

Today, that confidence appears increasingly fragile.

Americans haven't become weaker.

Too many institutions have become detached from the people they were supposed to serve.

The worker sees productivity rise while wages stagnate.

The student inherits debt before opportunity.

The family watches housing drift beyond reach.

The patient enters a healthcare system that often feels designed around billing codes rather than healing.

The veteran returns from war to discover gratitude is abundant while support remains scarce.

The citizen looks at the political system and sees influence increasingly concentrated among those already possessing wealth, access, and connections.

Then comes the most dangerous moment.

Not anger or outrage. No.

Resignation.

The belief that nothing can change.

The belief that participation no longer matters.

The belief that the outcome is predetermined.

The belief that the promise itself was always an illusion.

A republic can survive criticism.

In many ways, criticism strengthens it.

A republic can survive disagreement.

Disagreement is part of the design.

A republic struggles to survive cynicism.

Because cynicism is withdrawal.

It is the decision to stop investing.

To stop participating.

To stop believing that stewardship matters.

And once enough citizens arrive there, a vacuum appears.

Vacuums never remain empty.

Something always fills them.

Sometimes it is concentrated wealth.

Sometimes it is ideology.

Sometimes it is tribalism.

Sometimes it is spectacle.

Sometimes it is a charismatic figure promising simple answers to complex problems.

History has supplied endless variations.

The pattern remains remarkably consistent.

This is why I find myself increasingly frustrated by conversations that focus exclusively on individual personalities.

The names matter.

Of course they matter.

History is made by people.

Yet personalities alone cannot explain what happened.

No single politician created the conditions we now inhabit.

No single executive.

No single corporation.

No single movement.

The deeper betrayal occurred over decades.

One incentive at a time.

One loophole at a time.

One compromise at a time.

One surrender of responsibility at a time.

A culture that once admired builders began celebrating extractors.

A culture that once respected stewardship began rewarding optimization.

A culture that once produced citizens increasingly produced audiences.

The transformation was gradual enough to avoid alarm.

Until suddenly it wasn't.

Until communities weakened.

Trust weakened.

Institutions weakened.

The promise weakened.

And now, at two hundred and fifty years old, the American experiment finds itself facing a question every generation eventually confronts.

Not whether we have failed.

We have failed before.

Many times.

Not whether corruption exists.

Corruption has always existed.

Not whether greed exists.

Greed arrived with the first human beings and shows no signs of leaving.

The question is simpler:

Do we still possess the will to correct ourselves?

Because self-correction has always been America's true superpower.

Not wealth, military strength, geography, or resources.

The ability to look directly at our failures and choose something better.

That is the promise.

And that is the part we dare not lose.

## The Work Ahead

There is a temptation, when confronted with a story like this, to search for a hero.

Americans love heroes.

We always have.

We search for the next Washington.

The next Lincoln.

The next Roosevelt.

The next King.

The next figure capable of stepping forward, rallying the nation, and somehow fixing what feels broken.

The temptation is understandable.

It is also dangerous.

Because it quietly encourages us to believe that somebody else will do the work.

That somebody else will save the republic.

That somebody else will restore trust.

That somebody else will rebuild institutions.

That somebody else will heal the divisions.

That somebody else will step forward while we watch.

The truth is considerably less exciting.

And considerably more empowering.

The republic was never theirs.

It was always ours.

Every generation inherits the same responsibility.

Not to perfect the nation.

No generation has ever managed that.

Not to eliminate corruption.

No generation has ever managed that either.

The responsibility is simpler.

To leave the republic stronger than we found it.

To hand our children something worth inheriting.

To act as stewards rather than consumers.

To contribute more than we extract.

To build more than we dismantle.

To participate rather than merely complain.

The challenge is not theoretical.

It never has been.

It exists in school board meetings.

Town councils.

Volunteer organizations.

Church basements.

Little League fields.

Community colleges.

Neighborhood projects.

Food banks.

Veterans groups.

Conservation efforts.

Mentorship programs.

Local elections.

The places where citizenship still lives.

The places where ordinary people quietly perform extraordinary acts of stewardship every day without cameras, sponsorships, influencers, hashtags, or applause.

These places will never trend.

They will never dominate the news cycle.

They will never attract venture capital.

And yet they remain the foundation upon which everything else rests.

A republic is not ultimately sustained in Washington.

It is sustained in communities.

One conversation at a time.

One volunteer at a time.

One citizen at a time.

This truth may be disappointing to those searching for a dramatic solution.

It is also profoundly hopeful.

Because it means the future remains participatory.

The story is not finished.

The argument is not over.

The experiment continues.

Are enough of us willing to resume our role within it?

## America at 250

Two hundred and fifty years is a long time.

Long enough to accumulate triumphs.

Long enough to accumulate failures.

Long enough to build extraordinary institutions and then neglect them.

Long enough to create immense wealth and immense inequality.

Long enough to inspire billions of people around the world and disappoint millions at home.

Long enough to become both an example and a warning.

The American story contains all of it.

The builders and the grifters.

The reformers and the profiteers.

The visionaries and the opportunists.

The patriots and the demagogues.

The saints and the scoundrels.

Every generation contributes its chapter.

Ours will be no different.

The question is what we will choose to write.

Will we become another generation that inherited a remarkable experiment and slowly allowed it to erode through distraction, cynicism, tribalism, and neglect?

Or will we become the generation that remembered what citizenship was for?

The generation that rediscovered stewardship.

The generation that stopped confusing outrage for participation.

The generation that stopped waiting for heroes.

The generation that picked up the shovel.

History does not ask us to be perfect.

History rarely offers such luxuries.

It asks something far more achievable.

Pay attention.

Tell the truth.

Accept responsibility.

Honor the spirit of the agreement.

Leave things better than you found them.

The founders did not hand us a finished nation.

They handed us an unfinished promise.

For two hundred and fifty years, Americans have argued, struggled, sacrificed, fought, failed, corrected, rebuilt, and persevered in an effort to honor that promise.

Some succeeded magnificently.

Others failed spectacularly.

The work remained.

The work remains.

And as the republic turns two hundred and fifty years old, perhaps the most patriotic thing we can do is refuse both despair and complacency.

To love the country enough to criticize it honestly.

To believe in it enough to demand better from it.

To care enough to participate.

To understand that the promise was never guaranteed.

It was entrusted.

Entrusted to ordinary citizens.

Entrusted to imperfect people.

Entrusted to us.

The fireworks will fade.

The speeches will end.

The flags will be folded and stored away.

The birthday will pass.

The work will remain.

It always does.

And thank God for that.

Because as long as the work remains, so does the possibility.

And the possibility, more than anything else, is what America was always meant to be.
