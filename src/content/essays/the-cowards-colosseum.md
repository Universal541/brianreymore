A video came across my feed the other night.

You know the kind. Cell-phone footage. Shaky. Vertical. Too close to chaos to feel clean and too far from context to feel complete. A two-lane road backed up with traffic. Vehicles sitting still. People irritated. A protest blockade ahead. Some kind of trailer frame or obstruction across the road. Young protesters sitting on it, near it, around it. Calm, at least in the clip. Nonviolent, at least in the clip. Possibly breaking the law. Possibly not. The video itself did not explain everything, because videos almost never do.

Then a sheriff’s department pickup came tearing up the gravel shoulder, lights and siren going, full emergency posture. The truck appeared to ram the obstruction off the road. It swung around hard, climbed the opposite shoulder, and the officer jumped out hot, sidearm drawn, screaming.

“Get down on the ground!”

“Get down on the ground now!”

“Down!”

“Down!”

“I will shoot you!”

Then he moved in physically.

Maybe there is context missing. There usually is. Maybe the blockade was illegal. Maybe the officer had information not visible in the clip. Maybe someone had been warned. Maybe the department will argue necessity, public safety, obstruction, emergency access, or officer discretion. Maybe some of that will matter legally.

But the comment section did not wait for law. It did not wait for facts. It did not wait for a report, a court filing, a department statement, a civil-rights analysis, or even a basic human pause.

It laughed.

It cheered.

It mocked the protesters. It praised the officer. It fantasized about harsher force. It did what comment sections do now when the right people appear to be suffering: it turned into a little public arena, a cheap digital colosseum where ordinary citizens could sit in the stands, eat their algorithmic popcorn, and mistake contempt for conviction.

The problem was not merely that people disagreed with the protesters. Disagreement is allowed. Anger is allowed. If someone blocks a road, ordinary people trapped in traffic are allowed to be frustrated. A blocked road can keep a parent from a child, a worker from a job, an ambulance from a patient, a disabled person from safety, a family from home. Protest does not magically become wise because it is earnest. Civil disobedience has consequences. Strategy matters. Public sympathy matters. Law matters.

But none of that explained the glee.

That was the tell.

Because the comment section did not look like citizens wrestling with a hard public question. It looked like villagers who missed public hangings and finally got Wi-Fi.

That is where this essay begins. Not with the protesters. Not with the cop. Not with the road. With the spectators. With us.

Because the ugliest thing about social media is not that people say cruel things. People have always said cruel things. Human beings were gossiping, shaming, lying, mocking, scapegoating, bullying, exaggerating, tribalizing, and spiritually peacocking long before some haunted little rectangle started glowing in everyone’s hand.

Social media did not invent moral ugliness. It industrialized it. It gave cowardice a costume. It gave cruelty an audience. It gave resentment a scoreboard. It gave our worst impulses a distribution network and then called the whole thing “engagement.”

The algorithm did not make you an asshole.

It just handed your asshole a microphone, a scoreboard, and a cheering section.

That is the part we do not want to face. It is much easier to blame the platform, the billionaire, the algorithm, the political party, the media ecosystem, the other generation, the other ideology, the other religion, the other race, the other class, the other country, the other side.

And yes, all of those things matter. Platforms matter. Incentives matter. Algorithms matter. Billionaires matter. News collapse matters. Political manipulation matters. Foreign influence matters. Bots matter. Moderation policies matter. Censorship matters. Surveillance matters. Propaganda matters. Profit matters. The architecture matters.

But architecture does not absolve the occupant.

A casino is designed to exploit human weakness. That does not mean every drunk man at the slot machine has achieved moral purity because the carpet was ugly and the lights were manipulative. Social media is designed to reward reaction. That does not mean the person writing “run them over” under a video of protesters gets to pretend he is a helpless victim of interface design.

The machine matters.

So does the monkey.

And the monkey has been busy.

## The New Public Stoning

There is an ancient hunger in the online mob.

That sounds dramatic until you watch it closely. Someone posts a thought. Maybe it is wrong. Maybe it is clumsy. Maybe it is cruel. Maybe it is ignorant. Maybe it is just poorly worded. Maybe it is a joke that lands badly, a political opinion without sufficient tribal perfume, an old post dragged forward from a different season of a person’s life, a moment of fear, a moment of stupidity, a moment of pain, or a sentence that never should have left the drafts folder.

Within minutes, strangers arrive. Not to correct. Not to understand. Not even to argue. To punish.

They quote. They screenshot. They swarm. They diagnose. They label. They tag employers. They drag in family. They search old posts. They make threats. They laugh. They moralize. They posture. They call it accountability. Sometimes it is. Often it is not. Often it is just hunger wearing a judge’s robe.

The village square is back. Only now the village is global, anonymous, bored, monetized, sleepless, and armed with search history. A person can be tried, convicted, sentenced, mocked, fired, threatened, and reduced to one bad sentence before anyone involved has finished their coffee.

This is not justice. Justice has standards. Justice has evidence. Justice has proportion. Justice has process. Justice has memory and restraint and burden of proof and the possibility that facts are incomplete.

The mob has appetite.

That is the difference.

A mob is not a conscience just because it learned the vocabulary of justice.

This distinction matters because some public outrage is necessary. Some things deserve exposure. Some abuses should be named loudly. Some lies must be confronted. Some predators deserve no shelter behind politeness. Some institutions hide behind “civility” because they know civility can be weaponized against the people they have harmed.

So no, this is not an essay begging everyone to be nice while the powerful loot the house. That kind of niceness can go straight to hell with a gift basket. There are times when anger is not only appropriate but morally required. There are times when silence is complicity. There are times when the truth must be said hard, publicly, and without apology.

But moral courage is not the same as recreational cruelty.

Real accountability seeks truth, repair, protection, or proportionate justice. Performative outrage seeks status, dominance, entertainment, and belonging.

Real accountability asks, “What happened, who was harmed, what is true, what must change, and what would justice require?”

Performative outrage asks, “Who can I hate safely while people clap?”

That is the coward’s colosseum.

## Cruelty Wearing a Conscience Costume

The most dangerous cruelty in public life does not announce itself as cruelty. It announces itself as moral clarity.

That is why it spreads so easily. Nobody wants to think they are piling onto a stranger because they are bored, lonely, resentful, frightened, politically captured, spiritually empty, or hungry for status. That would require self-knowledge, and self-knowledge is much harder than typing “educate yourself” with the confidence of a drunk raccoon operating a forklift.

So the ego does what the ego does. It dresses the appetite in principle.

It says, “I am not humiliating this person. I am holding them accountable.”

It says, “I am not enjoying their fear. I am defending order.”

It says, “I am not joining a mob. I am standing with my community.”

It says, “I am not dehumanizing them. I am naming harm.”

It says, “I am not being cruel. I am being honest.”

Sometimes that is true.

Often enough, it is bullshit with a halo.

This is how people cheer excessive force against protesters and call it law and order. This is how people fantasize about violence against political opponents and call it patriotism. This is how people try to ruin someone’s life over a bad joke and call it justice. This is how people mock the suffering of strangers and call it consequences. This is how people turn disagreement into prosecution and then congratulate themselves for being brave.

But bravery requires risk. If you are surrounded by people who already agree with you, attacking someone they already hate, using language they already reward, while risking almost nothing, you may be doing many things. You may be bonding. You may be signaling. You may be venting. You may be entertaining yourself. You may even be correct.

But do not confuse that with courage.

Courage is telling the truth when your own side does not want to hear it. Courage is defending due process for someone you dislike. Courage is refusing to cheer when the boot lands on the other tribe’s neck. Courage is saying, “No, this is wrong,” even when the wrong person is being hurt. Courage is asking what actually happened before you grab a rock. Courage is remembering that constitutional rights do not become optional because the person exercising them annoys you.

Courage is not laughing while a stranger bleeds.

That is not courage.

That is cowardice with surround sound.

## The First Job of the Mob

The first job of the mob is to replace a human being with a category. After that, almost anything can feel justified.

Not a young person. A protester.

Not a frightened woman. A Karen.

Not a confused man. A boomer.

Not a scared cop. A pig.

Not a child. A thug.

Not a neighbor. A lib.

Not a worker. A fascist.

Not a person. A problem.

Once the label lands, conscience gets to clock out early.

This is the oldest trick in the human book. Social media did not invent it. Tribal politics did not invent it. Religion did not invent it. Governments did not invent it. They all just found uses for it. Every system that wants people to harm other people first teaches them to see a category instead of a face.

That is why the question matters: What if that protester were your daughter? What if that officer were your son? What if that person in the viral clip were your wife, your husband, your brother, your sister, your friend, your parent, your neighbor, your younger self?

This is not sentimental mush. It is moral technology.

Proximity restores weight.

Distance removes it.

Social media is an engine of distance pretending to be connection. It lets us see people constantly without having to feel the full reality of them. We see faces without relationship. Pain without obligation. Fear without consequence. Humiliation without aftermath. We see tiny fragments of human beings and then pretend the fragment is enough to justify the verdict.

That is how a comment section becomes a courtroom. That is how a stranger becomes a defendant. That is how we end up spiritually prosecuting one another all day long.

We are not merely disagreeing anymore. We are spiritually prosecuting each other. Every post becomes evidence. Every old sentence becomes a crime scene. Every stranger becomes a defendant in the tiny courthouse of our wounded little ego.

And the courthouse is busy. The docket is infinite. The judge is unstable. The jury is drunk. The bailiff is an algorithm.

## America Screamed Into a Mirror

Americans have become especially talented at moral suspicion.

Pew Research Center reported in 2026 that the United States was the only country in a 25-country survey where more adults described the morality and ethics of people in their own country as bad than good. In that survey, 53 percent of U.S. adults said Americans have somewhat or very bad morals and ethics.

That number is not just data. It is a national symptom. It says we no longer merely distrust institutions. We distrust each other at the level of character. We do not simply think our neighbors are wrong. We suspect they are morally rotten.

That is a dangerous place for a country to live.

Because once people believe their fellow citizens are not merely mistaken but corrupt, stupid, dangerous, hateful, brainwashed, evil, or beyond redemption, ordinary disagreement starts to feel like self-defense. Debate becomes contamination. Compromise becomes betrayal. Restraint becomes weakness. Due process becomes a technicality. Mockery becomes medicine. Force becomes fantasy.

This is how a republic becomes a comments section with borders.

Now, before anyone gets too smug, let us be clear: every generation has earned some of this contempt. Young people look around and say, “Adults are unethical.” Shocking. Angry young people assuming most adults are rude and corrupt is not exactly a new discovery. Every generation enters adulthood, looks at the world it inherited, and concludes that the adults before them were either fools, cowards, thieves, hypocrites, or some doomed casserole of all four.

Sometimes they are right. Sometimes they are just young. Often, both.

Gen X has no business pretending it came up in a monastery. We grew up in the 1980s, for God’s sake. We were raised in the decade of decadence, when greed got a movie-star slogan, cocaine capitalism wore suspenders, televangelists sweated money through pancake makeup, latchkey kids microwaved dinner under the glow of divorce, and half the country thought song lyrics were the thing destroying civilization while actual institutions were out back lighting the furniture on fire.

We had Cold War paranoia, AIDS hysteria, satanic panic, crack devastation, gang violence, PMRC pearl-clutching, Wall Street worship, Rambo fantasies, steroidal action gods, arcade violence, slasher movies, punk, metal, rap, rebellion-as-product, sex-as-advertising, and enough moral contradiction to choke a megachurch fog machine.

And yes, the Contra-era drug-trafficking record was ugly enough without needing careless shorthand. The Justice Department Inspector General’s review of the CIA-Contra-crack controversy summarized the original *San Jose Mercury News* allegations and also noted that the paper later clarified that the series did not report direct CIA involvement, even though many readers drew that conclusion. The broader historical record includes documented Contra drug links and official investigations that remain a permanent stain on the era’s foreign-policy and drug-war mythology.

So yes, kids, some adults are corrupt. Some always were.

But spare us the TED Talk from the generation that turned public shaming into cardio.

That is the irony. A young person can blast older generations as unethical while using rhetorical weapons forged by the very culture they condemn. They can listen to music descended from Gen X rebellion, consume aesthetics built out of old vulgarity, perform transgression in prepackaged templates, and then turn around and accuse everyone older than thirty-five of moral failure with the delicate humility of a parking-lot prophet on bath salts.

But this is not really about young people. It is not about Gen X. It is not about boomers, millennials, Gen Z, or whatever cursed label some consultant invents next so advertisers can sell identity back to people at a markup.

The young moral prosecutor and the old comment-section sadist are not opposites. They are often the same spiritual posture wearing different merch.

Every generation has its hypocrisy.

The platforms just made the hypocrisy searchable.

## The Global Machine

This problem is not only American.

Social media is now a planetary nervous system. DataReportal estimated that there were 5.79 billion social media “user identities” worldwide at the start of April 2026, while also noting that user identities are not the same thing as unique individual human beings. That still means the scale is staggering. A meaningful portion of the species now spends part of its waking life inside systems designed around attention, reaction, identity, and emotional movement.

Different countries use those systems differently. Different cultures bring different expectations about privacy, public dignity, authority, family, religion, humor, shame, law, protest, insult, honor, and the proper role of the state. But nobody gets to pretend the machine is neutral once it enters the bloodstream.

In the United States, social media often becomes identity combat. Politics, morality, race, sex, religion, patriotism, class, region, education, entertainment, and consumer preference all collapse into a single battlefield. Pew reported in 2025 that YouTube and Facebook remained the most widely used platforms among U.S. adults, while growing shares reported using Instagram, TikTok, WhatsApp, and Reddit. That matters because each platform has its own emotional weather. Facebook has the family-reunion knife fight. X has the public bathroom wall of empire. TikTok has the algorithmic carnival. Instagram has the curated insecurity aquarium. Reddit has the basement parliament. YouTube has the everything-machine.

Different rooms. Same species.

Europe has its own online ugliness, but many European countries have different instincts around regulation, privacy, hate speech, public order, and state responsibility. They are not magically enlightened. They have mobs, extremists, nationalists, trolls, conspiracy networks, influencer vanity, and comment-thread sewage like everyone else. But there is often a stronger expectation that digital platforms can and should be governed as public-risk infrastructure rather than treated only as private speech playgrounds.

Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and the United Kingdom occupy their own strange middle ground: culturally close enough to the United States to inherit much of the same platform sickness, but often more willing to discuss online harm as a public-safety issue. Sometimes that produces useful protections. Sometimes it produces overreach. Sometimes both arrive in the same legislative minivan.

In much of Latin America, Africa, South Asia, and Southeast Asia, social media is not merely “posting.” It is news distribution, small business infrastructure, family network, political organizing, rumor channel, entertainment system, emergency alert, religious pulpit, marketplace, and neighborhood gossip engine. A lie does not just go viral. It moves through family groups, churches, local communities, ethnic networks, activist circles, and political machines. A slander does not stay abstract. It can land in the street.

In more authoritarian environments, the danger changes again. The problem is not just mobs. It is surveillance, censorship, state manipulation, arrest, imprisonment, and fear. Freedom House reported in 2025 that global internet freedom declined for the fifteenth consecutive year and that people in at least 57 of the 72 countries it covered were arrested or imprisoned for online expression during the reporting period.

That contrast should shame the West more than it does. In some countries, a person risks prison to tell the truth online. In ours, a man uses the same miracle of speech to call a stranger trash because her take on a school-board meeting annoyed him.

Motherfucker, that is not freedom.

That is freedom with spiritual mange.

We were handed tools that dissidents, journalists, activists, religious minorities, reformers, whistleblowers, and ordinary citizens in repressive societies use at great personal risk. And what do we do with them? We threaten teenagers. We cheer state force when it lands on people we dislike. We post “educate yourself” under articles we did not read. We turn grief into branding. We turn politics into cosplay. We turn strangers into targets. We turn every tragedy into a loyalty test. We turn the public square into a bathroom stall and then act shocked when the smell changes the culture.

## The Machine Trains the Monkey

The platforms are not innocent.

Let us not drift into the fantasy that social media is merely a mirror. It is not. It is also a training device. A 2021 study in *Science Advances* found that positive social feedback for moral outrage expressions increased the likelihood of future outrage expressions, consistent with reinforcement learning. The study also found that users learn and conform to the outrage norms of their networks.

That is a polite academic way of saying the machine trains the monkey.

The monkey denies being trained.

Then the monkey writes “educate yourself” under a post it did not understand.

This is one of the great humiliations of modern life: people who think they are fiercely independent are often being shaped by tiny bursts of approval from strangers they would not trust to water a plant. A like is not just a like. A share is not just a share. A ratio is not just a ratio. A pile-on is not just a pile-on. These are behavioral signals. They teach. They reward. They punish. They tell users what kind of person gets applause in a given room.

If outrage gets attention, outrage grows. If contempt gets laughs, contempt grows. If cruelty gets belonging, cruelty grows. If nuance dies alone in the corner while mockery gets carried around the room on everyone’s shoulders like the homecoming queen of hell, then do not act surprised when fewer people choose nuance.

Platform design does not remove responsibility. It reveals how fragile responsibility was.

That is the brutal part.

We like to imagine ourselves as rational, grounded, self-governing citizens who use tools. Then the tool gives us a dopamine pellet for being a prick, and suddenly we are philosophers of necessary harshness.

No one is immune. Not me. Not you. Not the old guy laughing at protesters. Not the young activist hunting for a scalp. Not the patriot account posting eagle memes over threats. Not the justice account treating every disagreement as violence. Not the wellness influencer preaching compassion while subtweeting rivals into dust. Not the religious commentator praying in public and sneering in replies. Not the comedian confusing cruelty with courage. Not the journalist mistaking cynicism for insight. Not the intellectual performing complexity until someone unpopular needs defending. Not the brand account trying to sound human while emitting the spiritual odor of a conference room.

The machine finds the opening: vanity, loneliness, fear, boredom, status hunger, tribal longing, unresolved childhood humiliation, the desire to matter, the desire to win, the desire to punish someone safely.

It finds the tender little lever and starts pressing.

PTHLUMP.

There goes the ego.

## The Pleasure of Being Right in Public

The hardest part to admit is that outrage can feel good. It can feel clean. It can feel like energy returning to the body. Someone says something cruel, stupid, false, or dangerous, and suddenly the heat rises. The sentence forms. The blood moves. The target appears. The tribe gathers. The line lands. People approve. A small court convenes inside the chest and declares itself righteous.

I know this feeling.

I am not writing from some fake mountaintop of perfect restraint. I have felt the fire. I have answered cruelty with cruelty. I have called it truth because I was angry. I have confused accuracy with righteousness. I have swung the blade and only later wondered whether I was correcting the room or just enjoying the cut.

That admission matters because the point is not that “bad people” behave badly online. The point is worse. The machine does not only attract monsters. It trains ordinary people to enjoy monstrous little moments and then call them moral clarity.

That is what makes this so dangerous. Most people in the comment section are not cartoon villains. They are workers, parents, veterans, students, teachers, nurses, business owners, retirees, churchgoers, atheists, volunteers, neighbors, and people who probably help someone carry groceries when they are offline.

Then they log on and become spectators at the pain circus.

Why? Because social media creates distance without silence. It gives us access to other people without relationship to them. It gives us conflict without eye contact. It gives us judgment without responsibility. It gives us language without tone, speed without reflection, community without obligation, and punishment without due process.

That is a dangerous combination for creatures as insecure as we are.

## Law and Order for Thee

The protest video matters because it exposes one of the clearest moral fractures in American life: many people do not actually believe in rights. They believe in rights for people they approve of.

Free speech for my side. Consequences for yours. Due process for my side. Immediate punishment for yours. Police restraint when my people are scared. Force when yours are annoying. Public sympathy when my cause blocks traffic. Run them over when your cause does.

This is not principle.

It is tribal permission.

A constitutional right that depends on whether the crowd likes you is not a right. It is a favor. A law-and-order ethic that laughs at possible unlawful force because the target is unpopular is not law and order. It is appetite with a badge sticker. A civil-liberties ethic that excuses intimidation or violence when the correct activists do it is not civil liberty. It is appetite with a protest sign.

The line is not left versus right. The line is principle versus permission. And social media has made permission cheap.

If the person being harmed belongs to the wrong category, people grant themselves permission to stop caring. If a cop is hurt, one tribe laughs. If a protester is hurt, another tribe laughs. If a politician is threatened, people ask whether the politician deserved it. If a journalist is harassed, people check the journalist’s ideological scent before deciding whether harassment is bad. If an ordinary citizen is doxxed, people inspect the target’s opinions before locating their compassion.

This is moral rot. Not disagreement. Not politics. Rot.

A society cannot survive if its people only defend restraints when their own side benefits from them. That is not democracy. That is gang logic in business casual.

## The Insult Economy

Part of the sickness is that insult now functions as social currency.

A good insult travels. A brutal reply gets screenshotted. A dunk becomes a trophy. A ratio becomes a public beating with analytics. A cruel nickname can define a person more effectively than an argument ever could.

This is why so many online spaces feel like junior high school with monetization and geopolitics. People are not rewarded for understanding. They are rewarded for landing blows. They are not rewarded for asking better questions. They are rewarded for making the other person look small. They are not rewarded for proportion. They are rewarded for escalation. They are not rewarded for humility. They are rewarded for certainty performed at maximum volume by a nervous system running on caffeine, resentment, and unresolved daddy issues.

We built a global public square and immediately filled it with unpaid prosecutors, amateur executioners, brand managers, bots, and emotionally concussed raccoons with profile pictures.

That should embarrass us.

But embarrassment requires self-awareness, and self-awareness does not trend.

The insult economy is especially seductive because it feels like power for people who feel powerless. That matters. Many people are angry for reasons that are not imaginary. They have been lied to, priced out, ignored, mocked, surveilled, propagandized, manipulated, overworked, underpaid, spiritually starved, politically betrayed, medically bankrupted, algorithmically profiled, and sold a future that arrived pre-broken.

So yes, people are angry. They should be. But anger without discipline becomes fuel for anyone who knows how to harvest it.

Platforms know. Politicians know. Influencers know. Media companies know. Foreign intelligence services know. Extremist recruiters know. Scammers know. Brands know.

Everyone with a bucket is standing under the leak.

Your rage is useful to someone.

That does not make your rage false.

It makes it vulnerable.

## The Difference Between Anger and Rot

Anger can protect. Anger can clarify. Anger can move a person out of denial. Anger can say, “No more.” Anger can defend the vulnerable. Anger can expose corruption. Anger can force institutions to answer. Anger can be the immune system of conscience.

But anger can also become identity.

That is where it curdles.

When anger becomes identity, peace feels like death. Understanding feels like betrayal. Proportion feels like cowardice. Forgiveness feels like collaboration. Complexity feels like enemy propaganda. Every new outrage becomes proof that the self still exists.

This is why some people seem almost disappointed when a situation turns out to be less evil than they hoped. They wanted the hit. They wanted the clarity. They wanted the enemy. They wanted the little electric sacrament of being right again.

That is not justice.

That is addiction with a moral vocabulary.

And the dealer is open twenty-four hours a day.

## Western Intolerance and the Luxury of Recklessness

The West has a strange relationship with freedom. We talk about it constantly. We invoke it. We weaponize it. We print it on shirts, bumper stickers, campaign signs, beer cans, flags, and the sort of truck decals that make bald eagles look like they are about to repossess your soul.

But we do not always respect it.

We often treat freedom as the right to do whatever our side wants and the right to punish whatever the other side does. We confuse liberty with license. We confuse speech with insult. We confuse accountability with revenge. We confuse tolerance with weakness. We confuse intolerance with strength. And then we act surprised when people in other parts of the world look at Western online behavior and see decadence, childishness, arrogance, hypocrisy, or social decay.

Sometimes their criticism is self-serving. Authoritarian governments love pointing to Western chaos as proof that freedom is dangerous. They use our dysfunction to justify their control. That does not make them right. But it should make us less smug.

Because there is something obscene about abusing freedoms that others risk prison to exercise. There is something obscene about living in a society where we can criticize presidents, police departments, corporations, churches, schools, courts, agencies, newspapers, activists, and billionaires, then using that extraordinary freedom primarily to threaten strangers, dunk on teenagers, and turn every public event into a tribal food fight.

A person in a repressive society may risk everything to post one true sentence. A person here may use the same technological miracle to write, “Cry harder, bitch,” under a video of someone terrified.

That contrast is not flattering. It is not proof that censorship is better. It is proof that freedom without character decays into spectacle.

## The Generational Knife Fight

Every generation is now online accusing every other generation of ruining the world. Boomers ruined the economy. Gen X checked out. Millennials whined. Gen Z can’t cope. The next generation will probably accuse toddlers of insufficient climate solidarity before they have molars.

Some of the accusations contain truth. All of them become stupid when turned into total identity.

Generational critique can be useful. History matters. Policy matters. Economic timing matters. Cultural inheritance matters. One generation can absolutely leave another with debt, pollution, broken institutions, bad housing markets, weakened communities, moral confusion, and a music catalog that contains both genius and unforgivable crimes against the bass line.

But social media turns generational analysis into cartoon prosecution. Young people call older people corrupt. Older people call young people weak. Everyone screenshots the worst example they can find and uses it as evidence against millions of strangers.

This is lazy. It is also convenient. If an entire generation is the problem, you do not have to examine yourself. You just have to indict a birth cohort.

But the human problem crosses generations. There are selfish boomers and generous boomers. Checked-out Gen Xers and fiercely loyal Gen Xers. Entitled millennials and exhausted millennials. Hypocritical Gen Z activists and courageous Gen Z reformers. Cruel old men and cruel young women. Wise teenagers and foolish elders. Brave conservatives and cowardly conservatives. Brave progressives and cowardly progressives. Ethical atheists and corrupt believers. Saintly believers and smug secular sadists.

The categories are too blunt for the soul.

That does not stop us from swinging them around like shovels.

Social media loves categories because categories scale. A person is complicated. A category is efficient. The platform does not need you to understand. It needs you to react.

So the generational fight continues. Everyone guilty. Everyone innocent. Everyone wounded. Everyone prosecuting.

The courthouse of the ego remains open.

## What We Owe the Person We Oppose

There is a hard question underneath all of this: What do we owe people who are wrong?

Not people who are harmlessly mistaken. People who are actually wrong. People whose beliefs are destructive. People whose words are cruel. People whose politics frighten us. People whose activism obstructs us. People whose religion condemns us. People whose worldview disgusts us. People whose behavior causes real harm.

The answer cannot be “nothing.”

If the answer is nothing, then civilization is just a temporary cease-fire between tribes waiting for advantage. We owe them truth. We owe them proportion. We owe them enough process to prevent our own corruption. We owe them the moral discipline not to become recreational sadists just because they gave us a reason to be angry.

This does not mean we owe them agreement. It does not mean we owe them silence. It does not mean we owe them endless patience. It does not mean we owe predators access to prey. It does not mean we owe liars control of the record. It does not mean we owe violent people the benefit of naïveté.

It means we owe ourselves the restraint required to remain human while opposing them.

That is the part people miss. Restraint is not only for the target. It is for the person holding the weapon. Due process is not only for the accused. It is for the society that does not want to become a mob. Free speech is not only for the popular. It is for the moment when the unpopular person says something the powerful want buried.

Rights are not moral prizes. They are structural protections.

You do not test your belief in rights by defending people who flatter your worldview. You test it when the person irritates you. When they disgust you. When they inconvenience you. When the crowd wants blood. When the perfect insult is in your hand and your arm is already halfway through the swing.

## The Proportion Test

Maybe that is the test.

Not whether you can win online. Not whether you can land the perfect insult. Not whether you can gather applause from people who already agree with you. Not whether you can identify hypocrisy in your enemies. Any idiot can do that. Hypocrisy in the enemy is low-hanging fruit. It is practically lying on the ground wearing a reflective vest.

The real test is whether you can feel the old hunger rise and still choose proportion. Whether you can see a person you dislike being mistreated and refuse to celebrate. Whether you can criticize without dehumanizing. Whether you can be angry without becoming addicted to the shape anger gives you. Whether you can admit when your own side is wrong before the other side forces you to. Whether you can feel the perfect insult forming, know it would land, know people would clap, and still ask whether it serves the truth or just feeds the appetite.

This is not weakness.

It is harder than weakness.

Weakness joins the chant. Weakness hides in the crowd. Weakness lets the group supply the courage. Weakness waits until the target is safe to attack. Weakness calls cruelty justice because justice sounds better than fun.

Strength can stand alone. Strength can say, “That person is wrong, and this punishment is still excessive.” Strength can say, “That protest may be illegal, and the officer’s response may still be indefensible.” Strength can say, “That speaker is offensive, and censorship may still be dangerous.” Strength can say, “That institution has done harm, and lying about it will not heal anything.” Strength can say, “My side is acting like a pack of sanctimonious jackasses, and I will not pretend otherwise because the other side is worse.”

That is moral adulthood.

Which is probably why it is so rare online.

## The Line

The line is not between silence and outrage. Some things deserve outrage.

The line is not between civility and anger. Some civility is cowardice. Some anger is holy.

The line is not between kindness and confrontation. Some confrontation is the kindest thing left.

The line is between courage and cowardice.

Courage tells the truth while remembering the humanity of the person in front of it. Cowardice waits for a crowd, grabs a rock, and calls itself justice. Courage risks disapproval from its own tribe. Cowardice farms applause from people already hungry for the same target. Courage seeks what is true. Cowardice seeks what will land. Courage protects rights when they are inconvenient. Cowardice treats rights as rewards for ideological obedience. Courage confronts power without worshiping chaos. Cowardice worships force when it lands on the other side. Courage can hold anger without becoming possessed by it. Cowardice lets anger drive and then claims the wreck was necessary.

We have built a world where cowardice can look brave at scale.

That is the danger.

Social media did not make us cruel. It made cruelty efficient. It made contempt portable. It made humiliation participatory. It made moral vanity measurable. It made every person with a phone a potential spectator in someone else’s worst moment.

And now we have to decide what kind of spectators we are willing to be.

Because the next video will come. Another protest. Another arrest. Another school-board meltdown. Another celebrity collapse. Another crime. Another war clip. Another awkward teenager. Another panicked parent. Another stupid joke. Another frightened officer. Another grieving family. Another person having the worst day of their life in front of millions of strangers who need something to do while waiting in line.

The machine will offer us the same bargain.

React quickly.

Judge completely.

Laugh publicly.

Belong cheaply.

Punish safely.

It will hand us the rock. It will show us the crowd. It will tell us we are brave.

And somewhere in that moment, if there is still enough soul left in the room, we may need the absurd, wet, unmistakable sound of conscience arriving sideways like a dead salmon to the skull.

PTHLUMP.

Wake up.

That is a person.

That is a right.

That is a line.

Do not cross it just because the colosseum is cheering.
