The Stones We Carry
How Other People’s Shit Becomes the Architecture of Our Own Prisons
Someone at a barbecue tells you that you’ve always been a bit of a disappointment.
They say it while looking for the mustard.
This seems an inadequate setting for a verdict on your existence. You had imagined that any final assessment of your worth would involve a mountain, perhaps a tribunal, at least a chair facing the correct direction. Instead, there is a paper plate bending beneath a sausage, and someone has just pronounced sentence between the ketchup and the potato salad.
You laugh. Or freeze. Or produce a reply so feeble that your mind immediately establishes a commission to investigate it.
Three days later, in the shower, you deliver the response the occasion deserved. It is devastating. Measured. Almost Shakespearean, if Shakespeare had owned a pressure washer and understood the expressive range of the word fuck.
Unfortunately, your opponent is absent. They have been absent from every rehearsal. They may already have forgotten the exchange.
You have not. You are improving it.
By Thursday, the barbecue has acquired jurisdiction over your childhood. By Sunday, you are reviewing your career. Six months later, somebody offers you an opportunity and you hesitate, because a person who couldn’t locate the mustard has somehow become a recognized authority on your limitations.
A stone was thrown.
You have begun surveying for a wall.
Human beings are extraordinary collectors. We collect bottle caps, overdue notices, equipment we might repair, and remarks made in 1987 by people whose surnames we no longer remember.
Especially the remarks.
We keep them in remarkable condition. The car keys disappear daily, but the precise expression on a teacher’s face when she said we weren’t particularly bright remains available in high definition, with commentary.
Some of what we carry is far heavier than a careless insult. Betrayal. Humiliation. Abandonment. Years spent being diminished by someone whose love we needed. Injury inflicted by people or institutions with the power to make it stick.
Those stones did not arrive by invitation. Some struck before we had language for what was happening. Some broke things that have never healed properly.
But there is a further human complication: we can become custodians of the things that hurt us.
We examine them. Catalogue them. Carry them into new rooms. Hold them up against new people to see whether the shapes match. Eventually, with the patience of a medieval mason and none of the benefits of a medieval pension, we begin fitting them together.
This proves I’m unlovable.
This proves nobody can be trusted.
This proves I must never need anything.
An event becomes an explanation. The explanation becomes a rule. The rule begins making decisions.
And one day we discover that our life has acquired a peculiar floor plan. No windows on the side where disappointment might enter. No doors wide enough for dependence. A narrow passage for affection, subject to inspection.
We call it being realistic.
It may contain a great deal of reality. It may also contain the building specifications of someone who hurt us.
Memory has work to do.
Remembering a betrayal can help us recognize another. Remembering cruelty can keep us from returning to its source. A boundary often begins with the thoroughly sensible observation that the last time we opened this particular door, someone came through it carrying a shovel and a personal grievance against our skull.
The difficulty begins when a useful record becomes a constitution.
There is a considerable distance between this person betrayed me and betrayal is what intimacy means. Between I failed at something and failure is what I am. Between remembering that we were powerless then and assuming that we are powerless now.
The mind can cross that distance without announcing its departure.
Soon, a delayed reply is evidence. A disagreement is the opening act of abandonment. A kindness must conceal an invoice. We search the present for confirmation of the past, and the search can become so determined that the present has little opportunity to introduce itself.
We may withdraw before anyone can reject us. Test a relationship until it breaks under the testing. Refuse help and then stand among the wreckage of our exhaustion, taking bitter satisfaction in the fact that nobody helps.
Sometimes our suspicions are correct. People do repeat familiar harms. Discernment matters.
But a protective rule can survive the conditions that made it necessary. A wall built to withstand one siege may keep standing through decades of peace, while the people inside begin to mistake the lack of visitors for proof that the world is empty.
Nobody set out to build a prison. They wanted somewhere safe.
That is part of the tragedy. A structure can be understandable in its origins and unbearable to live in.
Any honest account must stop here long enough to look at what a metaphor can hide.
Some harms leave consequences that continue whether we think about them or not. A damaged body. Lost savings. A career derailed. A threat that remains active. A family or community still willing to defend the person who caused the harm.
No improvement in attitude pays the missing wages. No elegant sentence persuades an injured spine to reconsider.
Nor does a memory that returns uninvited mean someone has chosen to rehearse it. Fear, grief, and distress do not always wait for conscious permission. Telling a suffering person to stop carrying their stones can become another way of handing them one.
The question here concerns whatever room for movement remains—or can gradually be recovered—within those realities.
Who is permitted to tell the story of our worth? How much of the present must answer to an old injury? Which precautions still protect us, and which have begun to consume the life they were meant to preserve?
These are questions to approach with patience. Sometimes with help. Sometimes repeatedly, over years.
Agency can begin very small. A pause before obeying a familiar verdict. One person we allow to be different. One afternoon we spend doing something the injury did not choose.
There is no virtue in sneering at the size of an opening when someone has spent years trying to find one.
Then comes the problem of justice.
We want the person who hurt us to understand. We want acknowledgment specific enough to survive contact with the facts. An apology without that little trapdoor marked if you were offended.
We want someone to say: Yes. I did that. You did not invent it. You did not deserve it.
There is dignity in this desire. A truthful acknowledgment can return something that denial has stolen.
Unfortunately, the desire can also become an arrangement under which the least trustworthy person in our history is given sole authority to authorize our release.
I can move forward when they admit it.
Perhaps they will. Perhaps they have changed.
Perhaps they have spent twenty years developing an interpretation in which your principal offense was failing to appreciate how difficult it was for them to mistreat you.
We wait outside the office of their conscience. The lights are off. There is a fern dying in reception. We have brought enough evidence to convict a minor European monarchy.
Still, no one comes to the desk.
The waiting becomes a second relationship with them. It consumes time. Sustains arguments. Accompanies meals. They retain a prominent place in our lives despite having become, in every practical sense, someone we would cross a street to avoid.
Their apology might matter. It might repair part of what was damaged. But what if it never arrives?
That question can feel like a betrayal of justice. It asks us to imagine continuing to live while an account remains unsettled.
Yet an unsettled account does not require our continuous attendance. We can preserve evidence, pursue consequences, demand restitution, and protect others. We can refuse reconciliation. We can keep a door shut for the rest of our lives.
None of these actions requires us to hold an internal hearing every evening until death.
Attention is finite. Justice deserves some of it. So do the people we love, the work we might do, and the life that has continued arriving while we prepare our next statement to an empty room.
The stone falls. The landscape receives it. Nothing requires you to pick it up and spend the afternoon arguing with gravity.
There is something almost offensive about the simplicity of this.
The mind has prepared a substantial objection. There are exhibits. There is precedent. Surely the landscape should at least issue a condemnation.
But the landscape has other business. Rain is moving through it. Roots are finding their way. Somewhere, a beetle is engaged in a task it regards as indispensable.
The stone is there.
So is everything else.
That wider field is easy to lose. Injury narrows attention for understandable reasons. We look toward the threat. We study the place where the blow came from. But eventually the rest of life may have to stand waving at the edge of our vision, trying to remind us that it still exists.
Acceptance begins by allowing the fact to be a fact: this happened. It had consequences. Some remain.
Resignation adds a prediction: therefore nothing can change.
The distinction matters. Once we stop demanding that yesterday become a different yesterday, we may have more strength available for what can still be done today.
We can leave. Report. Grieve. Repair. Ask for help. Change the locks.
We can also eat breakfast without requiring the universe to settle its accounts before the toast goes cold.
Taoism, Stoicism, and Buddhist traditions differ in substantial ways. Treating them as interchangeable sources of soothing quotations does all three a disservice. Still, each offers a useful challenge to our habit of making life answer to an argument already underway in our heads.
A Taoist perspective invites us to notice the cost of forcing: the effort spent demanding that people, events, and outcomes conform to our insistence. Sometimes effective action becomes possible when we stop trying to push the river backward and attend to the current we actually face.
The Stoic distinction between what is ours to govern and what is beyond our command presses on another tender spot. We can influence other people, sometimes powerfully. We cannot guarantee their honesty, affection, judgment, or remorse. Building our peace around those guarantees leaves the foundation on property we do not own.
Buddhist nonattachment challenges the grip itself: the way we seize an experience, a verdict, a grievance, or a picture of ourselves and insist that it remain the organizing truth. Even the identity of the injured person can become something we feel obliged to defend.
These perspectives need not make us passive. They can help us act with less of ourselves tied up in demanding an impossible result.
You can object to cruelty without arranging your entire existence around the cruel person. You can feel anger without giving it a lifetime appointment as chief engineer. You can care deeply while conceding that caring does not grant command.
All of this sounds splendid until someone sends a text.
Then philosophy must leave the ornamental shelf and survive actual contact with your thumbs.
Suppose someone delivers a familiar little jab. You recognize the maneuver. Your body prepares for it before your most eloquent principles have found their trousers.
You feel hurt. Angry. Suddenly twelve years old.
Nothing has failed.
The useful question is what happens next.
Perhaps a response is necessary. Perhaps a boundary needs enforcing. Perhaps this is the moment to end a conversation you have been allowing to continue for too long.
Or perhaps you have answered this particular provocation often enough to know that another explanation will merely supply fresh material.
You let the message sit.
For a while, your mind keeps composing replies. You notice it. You return to what you were doing. Then you notice it again.
There is very little pageantry in this. No celestial choir. Nobody awards you a certificate in Advanced Detachment. You may remain annoyed while chopping an onion.
But the evening is still available.
That matters.
Freedom sometimes appears as an argument you decline to rehearse for the ninth time. A pleasant hour you allow to remain pleasant. A conversation with someone who is actually in the room.
Emotional suppression would require you to deny what you feel. This asks you to make room around the feeling, so it does not occupy every chair.
Forgiveness may come. It may not. Reconciliation requires its own reasons, including safety and trustworthy conduct. Access to you remains yours to decide.
There is no spiritual obligation to hand a repeat stone-thrower a better throwing position.
The hardest stones to set down are often the ones that seem to certify our experience.
If I stop carrying this, will anyone know what happened?
If I become happy, will it look as though it wasn’t serious?
If I no longer hate them, have they got away with it?
These questions reveal how suffering can become a form of testimony. We keep the wound on display because we fear the world will dismiss the crime if it sees us healing.
Sometimes the world has given us ample reason for that fear.
But a person’s recovery cannot retroactively make an injury acceptable. Joy does not acquit anyone. A good life does not falsify the record of what it cost to reach it.
We are allowed to keep the truth without continually reproducing the pain as proof.
Memory can become knowledge. I know what that behavior means. I know what I will do if it appears again. I know whom I can call. I know I am allowed to leave.
There is room in such knowledge for grief. There is room for anger when anger has work to do. There is also room for an ordinary Tuesday that concerns itself with other things.
Let it become part of the landscape, not the blueprint of your mind.
The stone remains somewhere along the path. You may recognize it when you pass. You may still feel something.
But you no longer have to carry it into every room you enter.
Perhaps years later you find yourself at another barbecue.
Someone passes you the mustard. A person you love is telling a story badly, taking a scenic route through details no competent editor would permit. There is smoke in your eyes. The food is nearly ready.
You could miss this.
For years, some part of you might have been elsewhere: improving your defense, awaiting a confession, trying to win a case against a person who had long since stopped attending.
Today, for a moment, you are here.
The past has not been corrected. You have not become invulnerable. Somewhere in the landscape are things you would never have chosen, and some of them still hurt.
But there is a voice beside you. There is food to share. There is a life asking for your presence.
You listen to the rest of the story.
Later, you may discover another stone in your hands. An old judgment. A familiar fear. You may have to learn its weight again before you realize you can put it down.
There is time for that.
For now, the people who hurt you have supplied quite enough material.
What you build next can begin with something else.
