The world is burning, and the footage is excellent.

The camera finds the orange sky. Someone adds a mournful piano. The caption asks whether humanity has finally gone too far, preferably in a font large enough to survive being watched without sound. Within minutes, the fire has been shared by people who are horrified, people who are delighted to have been right, and people who have already moved on to a video of a raccoon opening a refrigerator.

Then another fire appears.

I saw an image recently that asked whether we are genuinely interested in making the world a better place, or have simply become fascinated with watching it fall apart. It is the kind of question that can accuse everyone in the room, including the person who posted it. Especially the person who posted it. An image of a burning planet is still an image offered for our attention.

People who watch the news are not necessarily enjoying catastrophe. Some are trying to understand a threat. Some have family in its path. Some are looking for a way to help, and some are watching because what happened is so enormous they cannot yet work out what to do with it. Witnessing can be an act of care. A photograph can make denial more difficult; careful reporting can connect a distant decision to the people living with its consequences.

But fascination is real. So is the peculiar pleasure of saying, “See? I told you this would happen,” while standing safely outside the blast radius.

A disaster can become an episode. A political failure becomes a season finale. We learn the characters, argue about the plot, and await the next astonishing betrayal. We know exactly which public figure said something indefensible at 2:17 p.m. We know considerably less about who holds authority over the decision at issue, when that authority can be challenged, or what a better decision would require. The spectacle supplies endless proof that something is wrong. It seldom tells us where to put our hands.

That arrangement serves people who benefit from our exhaustion. An audience can be counted, targeted, sold to, frightened, and kept coming back. People who organize are more troublesome. They ask for records. They compare promises with decisions. They show up again after the cameras leave, often armed with a folder and a stubborn recollection of what was said at the last meeting.

The trouble begins when witnessing becomes the entire job: when we mistake being informed for having acted, or mistake our anger for a result. It is possible to spend the day studying an injustice so intently that the people working to correct it never hear from us. It is also possible to turn every attempt at repair into another occasion for proving that repair is futile. After enough repetitions, despair can begin to sound like sophistication.

We should be honest about why that happens. Most of the problems arriving on our screens are bigger than any one person’s reach. A citizen cannot personally settle a war, rebuild a health system, or persuade a burning forest to reconsider. “Do something” can sound like an insult when the scale of the damage is so large and the available handles are so hard to find. Many people are already using every bit of strength they have to keep a household intact. They need no lecture from someone whose chief contribution is typing *DO SOMETHING* beneath a photograph of smoke.

And yet the distance between *I cannot fix everything* and *there is nothing I can do* is where a great deal of public power quietly disappears.

The useful question is rarely “How do I save the world by Thursday?” Thursday has other plans. A better beginning is to ask who made a decision, where its record can be found, who must live with it, and who has already been working on the problem longer than we have. Perhaps there is a meeting, a deadline, a local counterpart, or an alternative that needs an extra pair of hands. Perhaps what is required is less dramatic: help a neighbor get through the week, tell the truth about an error, keep a small promise, or build something sturdy enough to be useful when the noise has passed.

The machinery that delivers catastrophe to our screens has little reason to show us such work. A resident who stays through a tedious public meeting until an official answers a question makes poor television. So does the worker who documents a dangerous practice, the person who drives someone to an appointment, or the neighbor who repairs a step before somebody falls through it. These acts lack a score. They also have a better chance of changing what happens next than our most eloquent declaration that everything is doomed.

None of this requires cheerful denial. Some things are getting worse. Some losses cannot be repaired, and some people are asked to bear far more than their share. Hope loses credibility when it refuses to look at the damage. It earns a little back when it looks closely, learns the terrain, and decides where to begin.

This is also where gratitude enters, though it ought to be handled carefully. Gratitude is sometimes used as a gag: be thankful for what you have, stop making trouble, and please do not mention the leaking roof while we are carving the turkey. That is a remarkably convenient definition for anyone responsible for maintaining the roof.

I mean something more demanding. I am grateful for people who still tell the truth when a lie would be easier. For those who bring food, lend tools, keep records, teach a child, make room at a table, or return to a hard conversation after everyone else has declared it hopeless. I am grateful that we can still find one another amid the racket, and that the future has not yet been decided by the loudest people narrating its collapse.

A holiday meal will not extinguish a forest fire or cure a broken institution. It can remind us why either is worth the effort. The people around a table are more than an audience for our opinions. They are lives that continue after the screen goes dark, with needs and gifts and stories we may miss if we keep looking past them toward the next alarming glow.

So this Thanksgiving, I hope we can look squarely at what is wrong without surrendering our appetite for what is good. May there be food enough to share, room for those who are grieving, patience for the complicated people we love, and gratitude that does not require anyone to pretend. May we leave the table a little more willing to protect what sustains us and a little better prepared to help where our hands can reach.

Happy Thanksgiving, one and all.
