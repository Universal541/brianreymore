I believe in entrepreneurship. I believe in innovation, private enterprise, competition, and rewarding people who take risks and build something worthwhile.

What I don't believe in is treating capitalism like a fucking religion whose wealthiest practitioners are automatically its high priests.

And I certainly don't believe that questioning the behavior of billionaires makes someone jealous, lazy, hateful, or evil.

That's not an argument. That's intellectual surrender dressed up as economic philosophy.

Let's get something straight.

**Capitalism is an economic system. It is not a moral authority.**

Its legitimacy comes from how well it serves human beings, not how efficiently it concentrates wealth and power in the hands of a few.

Peter Barnes explored this beautifully in his 2006 book *Capitalism 3.0: A Guide to Reclaiming the Commons*.

His argument, in essence, is that capitalism needs an upgrade—one that recognizes the natural resources, public institutions, shared knowledge, and inherited wealth of civilization upon which private enterprise depends.

Because nobody builds a multibillion-dollar empire entirely by themselves.

Not Elon Musk. Not Jeff Bezos. Not Mark Zuckerberg. Nobody.

They build upon generations of publicly supported research, infrastructure, education, technological development, and the work of enormous numbers of other human beings.

Their employees don't merely help them become wealthy.

**Their employees help create the wealth.**

And the public helps create the conditions that make that wealth possible.

Now, here's a little American economic history that seems to have escaped the billionaire-worshipping congregation.

During much of the 1950s and early 1960s, the highest federal marginal income-tax rate was 91%.

Ninety-one fucking percent.

That didn't mean wealthy people surrendered 91% of everything they earned. It applied to income above the highest tax threshold, and effective rates were considerably lower.

But here's what matters.

**Some of the highest marginal income-tax rates in American history existed alongside one of the most extraordinary periods of sustained economic expansion the country had ever experienced.**

Businesses expanded. Productivity increased. The middle class grew. Workers purchased homes, raised families, and participated in an extraordinary period of economic development.

That prosperity was neither universal nor the product of any single economic policy. Industrial development, organized labor, public investment, technological progress, international conditions, and countless other forces contributed to it.

But those extraordinarily high marginal rates were part of the economic landscape.

And wealthy Americans continued accumulating wealth.

So perhaps we can dispense with the fairy tale that taxing the wealthiest Americans at substantially higher rates necessarily brings economic civilization crashing down around our ears.

Now let's examine something considerably more interesting.

**Who actually receives the wealth that an enterprise creates?**

Because not all employees occupy the same economic universe.

At the upper levels of successful technology companies, highly specialized engineers, executives, and other strategically important employees can command extraordinary compensation packages, including salaries, bonuses, and substantial equity.

Companies compete fiercely for these people because their knowledge, relationships, and continued participation may be enormously valuable.

Move down the organizational hierarchy, however, and the picture can change dramatically.

Warehouse workers. Delivery drivers. Maintenance personnel. Customer-service representatives. Food-service workers. Contractors. The people who keep the physical and operational machinery functioning.

Their bargaining power, access to equity, job security, and ability to negotiate compensation can be vastly different.

Yet their labor remains indispensable.

A company cannot deliver products without the people who move them. It cannot operate facilities without the people who maintain them. It cannot serve customers without the people who do the serving.

**Indispensable work does not always command indispensable wages.**

And that is one of the central moral contradictions of modern capitalism.

According to the Economic Policy Institute, between 1979 and 2025, productivity increased at an annualized rate of 1.4%, while typical hourly pay increased just 0.6%.

Workers became substantially more productive, but typical compensation did not keep pace.

The benefits of economic growth have not been distributed evenly.

And those disparities become particularly troubling when the people with the least economic leverage are also those struggling to afford housing, food, transportation, healthcare, and the ordinary necessities of a dignified existence.

Let's be clear about something.

**A wage is not morally adequate simply because an employer can find someone desperate enough to accept it.**

The market may establish a price for someone's labor.

It does not establish the worth of the human being performing it.

And when a company generates extraordinary wealth while people performing essential work within its operations cannot afford a decent standard of living, that is a legitimate subject of moral and economic scrutiny.

It is not envy.

It is not hatred.

And it certainly isn't an attack on entrepreneurship.

Now consider our modern technology billionaires.

Some are extraordinarily talented entrepreneurs and innovators. Their accomplishments deserve to be examined on their merits.

But technological brilliance, business success, and enormous personal wealth do not automatically confer wisdom, maturity, ethical judgment, or a mandate to redesign human civilization.

Yet we've created an economic environment in which a relatively small number of extraordinarily wealthy individuals can exercise enormous influence over communication, employment, information, infrastructure, and public policy.

Their personal visions can become society-wide experiments, often without anything resembling democratic consent.

And we're supposed to applaud because they're rich?

Bullshit.

A billion dollars is not a philosophy degree.

A hundred billion dollars is not a moral credential.

And being able to build a rocket doesn't mean you understand what makes a civilization worth inhabiting.

The problem isn't wealth itself.

The problem is **wealth becoming so concentrated that private ambition can overwhelm public accountability.**

Meanwhile, we're told that people receiving public assistance are parasites.

Interesting.

Public investment helps educate the workforce, build infrastructure, fund research, protect property, and establish the legal systems upon which private wealth depends.

Businesses may also receive subsidies, tax incentives, and other forms of public support.

But when assistance helps a family buy groceries, suddenly we're supposed to believe civilization is collapsing under the weight of freeloaders.

What a remarkably convenient moral distinction.

And here's the part that seems to disappear from these conversations entirely.

**Human beings are not merely economic units.**

We are not born to spend our lives demonstrating that we deserve to exist by generating sufficient profit for somebody else.

We are people.

We have families, relationships, aspirations, vulnerabilities, communities, and lives that extend beyond the hours we sell to an employer.

An economy that generates extraordinary wealth while leaving substantial numbers of the people who sustain it struggling to live with dignity has a problem that cannot be explained away by pointing to its balance sheets.

Because the purpose of civilization is not to maximize the net worth of its wealthiest participants.

It is to create conditions in which human beings can flourish.

I don't want to destroy capitalism.

I want an economic system mature enough to recognize that **human prosperity is the purpose of economic activity—not an inconvenient expense to be minimized in pursuit of private fortunes.**

Markets are tools.

Corporations are institutions.

Money is a medium of exchange and a measure of certain kinds of value.

None of them is sacred.

Human dignity, opportunity, community, environmental stewardship, and the ability of future generations to flourish deserve a place at the center of our economic decisions.

If our version of capitalism cannot reliably serve those purposes, then it needs to evolve.

Not because capitalism is inherently evil.

Because no economic system deserves permanent immunity from improvement.

And if the only defense someone can offer for the existing arrangement is that its critics are jealous, lazy, or hateful, perhaps it's time to stop worshipping the machinery long enough to examine what the fucking machinery is actually producing.

**People before profit.**

Not because profit is bad.

Because profit is supposed to serve people.

Not the other way around.
