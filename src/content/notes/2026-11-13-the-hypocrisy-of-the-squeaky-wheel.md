# The Hypocrisy of the Squeaky Wheel

A man with a Southern drawl recently pointed out the peculiar sight of someone wearing $150 sneakers, speaking into a $1,000 phone, and declaring themselves oppressed.

It’s a good line. You can almost hear a porch chair creak beneath it. And there are people who ought to hear it, especially those who mistake the absence of applause for persecution and a mildly unpleasant interaction for a historic injustice.

But a good line can become a lazy argument if we let it do all the thinking.

The phone may be financed. The shoes may have been a gift. Neither item tells us what happened at work, what the landlord did, or what it costs that person to walk through a world we experience differently. You cannot audit a stranger’s life by glancing at their feet.

You *can*, however, ask what they mean by oppression. That word ought to carry more weight than a customer-service complaint.

Some people have grown remarkably skilled at turning comfort into grievance. They have a platform, an audience, a wardrobe selected for the occasion, and the conviction that every closed door is a personal attack. If someone disagrees with them, they’ve been silenced. If a rule applies to them, they’ve been targeted. If they don’t get the outcome they wanted, the entire system must be rigged.

Sometimes a system *is* rigged. That is precisely why we should resent the people who use its language to describe a bruised ego. They consume the attention needed by someone whose wages were stolen, whose care was denied, or whose complaint disappeared into an office that knows nobody will come looking for it.

The squeaky wheel is supposed to alert us to a problem. These days, some wheels come fitted with a microphone, a merchandise table, and a subscription link. They have learned that outrage draws a crowd. Why stop squeaking long enough to fix anything when the noise is paying the bills?

And before the man on the porch gets too comfortable, he has some explaining to do as well.

It is wonderfully convenient to judge another person’s hardship by the nicest thing they own. It saves us the trouble of asking what happened. Better still, it lets us feel wise while knowing almost nothing. A phone becomes a verdict. A pair of sneakers becomes a complete economic history. Poverty, apparently, is only credible if you arrive barefoot and communicate by smoke signal.

That, too, is the hypocrisy of the squeaky wheel: demanding that other people justify their complaints while treating our own irritation as self-evidently righteous.

We need a better test than the price of somebody’s shoes or the volume of their voice. What happened? Who had power? What harm was done? Can it be shown? What would repair it? And is the person making the noise willing to share the wheel when someone else has a stronger claim?

A complaint can be legitimate even when its owner has nice things. It can also be ridiculous when its owner has very little. Hardship is not a costume, and outrage is not proof.

Listen when the wheel squeaks. Then look underneath the cart. Sometimes there’s a broken axle. Sometimes there’s a stone in the road. And sometimes somebody installed a foghorn because it was easier than learning to steer.
